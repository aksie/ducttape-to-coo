# Company-Level Planning & Goals — Growth (~50–150 people)

> At growth stage, planning is an **operating rhythm** across horizons — not a quarterly goal list and not strategy craft. This page is the **planning cycle** (cadence, resourcing, who owns the process); strategy content belongs elsewhere. Builds on [1.1 @ early revenue](1.1--early-revenue.md) for goal cascade and named ownership.

## What good looks like at this stage

- **Planning operates as a cadence system across time horizons: week, month, quarter, year, strategy, vision, and mission.**
  Growth adds recurring planning events that cannot be coordinated from a single quarterly goal list. Cadence turns planning into an operating rhythm.
  <!-- sources: src-024 (GitLab) -->

- **Quarterly planning has a concrete rhythm** — ~3 weeks before the team planning day, revisit strategy and year objectives and set **3–5 quarter projects**, each fully staffed. Mid-quarter check at ~5–6 weeks. Weekly management-team review is **issue-based** — movement and bottlenecks only, not status theatre on green work.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **The COO (or equivalent) owns the planning process** — capacity, priority enforcement, templates, and comfortable "no"; sits closer to the CFO than the CEO. The **CEO owns ambition and story**, not daily reprioritisation or being process police.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Big efforts span multiple quarters with named phases** — advance the next phase each quarter instead of discarding and restarting. Example: three margin-improvement phases across three quarters that sum to the goal; if phase two slips, it runs another quarter.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Company roadmap and product roadmap stay coupled but not identical** — same top priorities and capacity reality; BAU, HR cycles, events, and fundraising sit outside the product roadmap but inside company planning.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Running metrics (company top line — MRR, NPS, eNPS, etc.) are set from C-level with feasibility input; change goals are owned locally.**
  Pick metrics that fit your business — practitioners often use MRR, NPS, or eNPS at the top line. Each department owns three to five real problems or chances at year horizon, then quarter — complementing top-line metrics with owned local improvement agendas.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) | flags: conditional -->

- **Departments pre-book only ~70% of capacity on their own objectives** — reserving slack for cross-team dependencies (marketing ↔ engineering ↔ legal).
  When two departments collide, C-level resolves priority. The ~70% figure is a practitioner rule of thumb, not a universal benchmark.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) | flags: conditional, needs practitioner check -->

## What you actually need to do

1. **Before changing planning rhythm, name who owns the process and who maintains it.**
  A process is an efficiency move to get relevant information to decision-makers — changing frameworks without ownership usually reverts within a quarter.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) | flags: needs practitioner check -->

2. **Each quarter: pick strategic projects, assign owners, and staff them — or admit you cannot.**
  Resource planning is the step that, if skipped, breaks everything immediately.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

3. **Weekly leadership: walk strategic projects — deep-dive only where stuck; skip green.**
  It is better to have a quick informal mental walk-through of every project than have stoplight dashboards that nobody trusts.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

4. **Keep the product roadmap aligned to company priorities** — scoping a major new feature can itself be a 1–2 month project with business stakeholders; sales, platform, and GTM cycles must stay visible outside the product view alone.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

5. **Calibrate planning detail to project maturity** — a still-vague bet should not be forced into a heavy template; a well-understood one should be.
  In this example, making the scoping or shaping of the bet (in the Shape Up methodology) the project.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

6. **When a bet would disrupt the quarterly plan, the role-owner escalates for capacity; the CEO weighs the trade-off** — Once priorities are set, the COO-owned rhythm should run. The CEO sells ambition and story, not daily reprioritisation ("wait, let's do something else"). The designed exception: the accountable domain owner says "this is huge, I need capacity"; the CEO then decides whether the plan absorbs the disruption — not the other way around.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

## Warning signs you're behind

### Output quality

- **Strategic debate lands in a weekly tactical meeting** — unprepared room, rushed decisions, bad outcomes.
  Wrong meeting layer wastes executive time and forces choices without context or data.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) -->

- **Departments run at 100% local load on their own objectives** — permanent gridlock on shared dependencies.
  Cross-team work needs unbooked capacity; fully booked departmental plans cannot absorb interdependencies.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) -->

- **Quarterly planning without resource planning** — picking priorities nobody can staff will fail immediately; the quarter looks full on paper and empty in reality.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Twenty "priority" projects and no execution discipline is bad** — because when everything is priority one, nothing moves; 3–5 resourced quarter bets is the corrective pattern.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Each quarter throws the previous project away instead of advancing its next phase** — multi-quarter work gets reset every ninety days instead of compounding.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

### Founder / key-person time

- **Around eighty people, every department plans differently — and executives keep re-deciding things the CPO, CRO, or other domain head should own.**
  That usually means planning was never clearly owned below the executive team. Headcount will vary — the pattern is uneven planning plus executive override, not a magic number.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) | flags: conditional -->

### Process entry

- **The company only does planning once per quarter and everything else is ad hoc.**
  Without a cadence, important planning events (strategy review, vision check, annual planning) get squeezed out by the urgency of the quarter.
  <!-- sources: src-024 (GitLab) -->

## How this evolves next

- **OKRs need to sit inside a broader company cadence with board meetings, sales targets, metrics reviews, yearly planning, strategy, vision, and mission.**
  OKRs coordinate goals, but they do not coordinate every recurring management process. A growing company needs both the goal system and the operating calendar to fit together.
  <!-- sources: src-024 (GitLab) -->

- **At scaled stage, stretch project horizons on the same rhythm — and make the planning/COO function permanent** — Don't abandon the quarterly cycle, mid-quarter check, or issue-based weekly MT walk; scale it. Strategic bets routinely span multiple quarters, so the informal founder walk-through of every project stops working. A standing planning or COO role — closer to capacity and priority enforcement than to strategy craft — becomes non-optional: someone must own the system, trade-offs, and comfortable "no." For what changes next: [1.1 at Scaled](1.1--scaled.md) (maintaining the planning hierarchy) and [1.4 at Growth](1.4--growth.md) (executive handover — projects, not tasks).
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), src-024 (GitLab) -->

## Tools & resources

- **Issue-based project review in weekly MT** — each week: update, movement, bottleneck? Skip what runs fine. Practitioner experience: stoplight/RAG dashboards for everything often become theatre; walking projects verbally worked better.
