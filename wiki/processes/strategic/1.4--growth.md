# Who Decides What — Growth (~50–150 people)

> At growth stage, C-level leaders sit in two teams at once — their function and the company. The job is to align those allegiances, not to carry the functional team's vote into the room. This builds on founder alignment and written decision rules from [First Hires](1.4--first-hires.md) — growth adds executive-scale mechanics; it does not replace them.

## What still applies from earlier stages

Growth adds C-level rules. It does not replace what you built at 2–25 people:

- **Named decider + duty to consult before calling it** — still the default for reversible (Type 1) calls. The decider gathers input from affected people and relevant expertise, then decides — not a vacuum decision. See [1.4 — First Hires](1.4--first-hires.md).
  <!-- sources: src-063 (synthesis), src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **ABC decision map among founders** — Bucket A (domain owner decides after consult), B (discuss + tie-break), C (unanimous). At growth, extend the same logic to executives and written escalation thresholds, not just founders.
  <!-- sources: src-063 (synthesis) -->

- **One visible approval / escalation list** — what still needs founder or exec-team OK (hiring, spend above €X, pivots) vs what the domain owner decides alone. If the team still waits for founder OK on everything, fix that before adding Type 1/2 process.
  <!-- sources: src-063 (synthesis), src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) -->

- **Accountability requires authority** — if someone owns an outcome, they must be able to decide on it. Executive handover from day one (below) is the growth-scale version of delegate projects, not tasks.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Codify decisions that matter within ~48 hours** — decision rights and [1.3 Communication Norms](1.3--first-hires.md) fail together when everything stays oral or in chat.
  <!-- sources: src-063 (synthesis) -->

## What good looks like at this stage

- **Reversible (Type 1) and non-reversible (Type 2) decisions are explicitly described mini processes** — reversible (Type 1) decisions delegate to the role-owner within their mandate; irreversible, cross-cutting bets (Type 2) use an integrative process where a role-owner can block only with a **reasoned objection** (e.g. CFO: action makes solvency impossible), not "I don't like it." Absorb Holacracy's objection principle without the full-system theatre.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Prefer "I've been heard" over harsh disagree-and-commit** — the CEO can still decide and ask for execution commitment: "I hear you no longer agree, but we're doing it — I need your commitment to execute."
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Leaders play for the highest team, not the lowest** — e.g. a CRO aligns commercial-team allegiance with C-level company allegiance. Open disagreement with the CFO can be healthy; secretly carrying the functional team's vote into the C-suite is not.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) | flags: needs practitioner check -->

- **When C-level debates something outside their domain, it returns to the domain owner** — e.g. "This doesn't touch revenue; it's the CPO's call." C-level time should go to cross-cutting decisions, not re-litigating operational choices that belong to domain owners.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) -->

- **On multi-sided platforms, consider a neutral coordinator for the cross-cutting outcome** — when dev, marketing, and monetization each own a slice, don't ask one side to write the others' strategy. Authority and accountability for the combined outcome may need a role above the functions — practitioners sometimes call this "traffic control."
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Structurally opposed roles stay split** — CEO and CFO (or comparable) are not one person: the CEO holds compromise and alignment; the CFO holds the hard line on constraints. Same pattern for pricing (P) vs volume marketing (Q) — they must talk well, not merge.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Recreate "small-room magic" deliberately** — clear roles plus a maturity and trust layer: the right people on their domain whom you respect and who will push harder than you would. Structural clarity without trusted domain owners still fails.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

## What you actually need to do

1. **Coach that owning a decision means checking cross-effects first** — "Did you check with the platform owner what this means?" If not, back to the drawing board. People fear solo decide; in practice consultation happens when the norm is clear.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

2. **Document what qualifies as a Type 2 objection** — for irreversible, cross-cutting bets, write down per role what lets them block — and what doesn't. **CFO example:** an action that would make monthly solvency impossible — a core mandate and the baseline safety the company depends on them to protect. "I don't like it" and "I have a better idea" don't qualify; a valid block must be reasoned and worked into the solution.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

3. **When you hire an executive, hand over the role from day one** — give them real decision rights in their domain and let them run it in their style. Don't keep them on a leash through months of small shadow projects while you retain the final call.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

4. **Before assigning a cross-cutting role, check three things:** Can this person take the authority (skill, maturity)? What other hat do they wear (will they exercise the role through one lens)? Do we trust them on this domain? Assign **missions**, not permanent identity — interim VP, six-month mission to stand up a function.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

5. **When three teams are each locally right but the platform outcome fails, ask whether cross-cutting ownership is missing** — don't bolt coordination onto one function's extra hat. Naming a neutral owner for the combined outcome is one pattern that works on multi-sided platforms; it's not required for every company at this stage.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

## Warning signs you're behind

### Output quality

- **C-level executives defend their function as if representing a team vote** — negotiations drip down the org instead of resolving at the right level. Functional advocacy in the C-suite prevents company-level tradeoffs.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) -->

- **People-pleasing managers optimise locally** — common with internal promotions and external hires, often from good intentions — and the company pays in gridlock on cross-team priorities.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) -->

- **Two structurally opposed roles collapsed into one person** — CEO + CFO in one head, or the pricing owner also driving volume marketing. Both mandates degrade.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Cracks between teams** — dev, marketing, and monetization each locally right; platform wrong globally. Signal that traffic control is missing or weak.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

### Founder / key-person time

- **The same high performers sit on every cross-functional project and every BAU optimisation** — time squeeze and "which hat" squeeze on your best people with no clean structural answer.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

### Process entry

- **"Careful onboarding" as a euphemism** — a senior hire drifts through small projects for four months, then is judged "slow to start." Often failure to hand over the role, not a bad hire.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Full framework rollouts before the org is ready** — the team spends more time keeping the process running than moving the business forward. Frameworks like Holacracy contain useful ideas — integrative objection, explicit decision rights — but importing the whole rulebook at this stage can be too burdensome. Then it might be better to extract the principles and adapt them to how your team already works.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

## How this evolves next

- **Accept permanent imperfection on org design** — the people good enough for strategic cross-functional work are often the same people carrying BAU optimisation; manage the squeeze explicitly rather than chasing one perfect org chart.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **At scaled stage**, wrap process steps around founder intuition and use structured conflict tools — see [1.4 at Scaled](1.4--scaled.md).
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) -->
