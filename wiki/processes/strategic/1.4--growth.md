# Who Decides What — Growth (~50–150 people)

> At 50–150 people the question stops being "who decides" and becomes "who decides what, and who has to be asked first." Your executives now sit in two teams at once — their own function and the company — and the company loses whenever someone carries the function's vote into the room. Nothing you built at 2–25 people gets replaced here; growth adds executive-scale mechanics on top of it.

**Five ideas hold at every stage. Growth only changes the mechanics:**

1. **Accountability and authority travel together.** Whoever owns an outcome can decide on it — otherwise the team waits, and you end up back inside decisions you delegated.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->
2. **Decide who decides before you need it.** Rules written mid-conflict read as a power move; the same rules written in calm are just a map you look up.
   <!-- sources: src-063 (synthesis) -->
3. **A decision you can walk back deserves less process than one you can't.** Cheap to reverse: one owner, light consult, move. Expensive or impossible to reverse: more people, more time, an explicit close.
   <!-- sources: src-063 (synthesis), src-070 (serial-ceo) -->
4. **Consult, then decide — don't decide by committee.** One named person owns the call and has to gather input from the people affected and the people with relevant expertise first. Gathering input is not the same as needing everyone to agree.
   <!-- sources: src-063 (synthesis), src-070 (serial-ceo) -->
5. **Formalise only when the informal version visibly breaks.** Process added ahead of the pain costs more than it saves.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

**Carried over unchanged from earlier stages:** the ABC decision map (domain owner decides after consulting / discuss then tie-break / unanimous), one visible list of what still needs founder or exec-team OK — hiring, spend above €X, pivots — and writing down decisions that matter within about 48 hours, because decision rights and [communication norms](1.3--first-hires.md) fail together when everything stays oral. The detail lives in [1.4 — First Hires](1.4--first-hires.md). If your team still waits for founder OK on everything, fix that first; none of the executive mechanics below work on top of it.
<!-- sources: src-063 (synthesis), src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) -->

## What good looks like at this stage

- **Two decision paths, and everyone knows which one they are on.** Decisions the team can walk back — a vendor, a campaign, a way of working — sit with the role owner inside their mandate: consult, decide, move. Decisions that are expensive to walk back, or that bind other functions to them, run a short shared process instead: everyone affected is heard, and a role owner can stop it only by naming a concrete way it breaks something they are accountable for — not "I don't like it." That last rule is worth borrowing from Holacracy, a complete self-management system with its own rulebook, without taking the rest of it.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Disagreement closes on "I've been heard," not on agreement.** The CEO can still decide against a dissenting executive and ask for delivery: *"I hear that you no longer agree. We're doing it, and I need your commitment to execute."* That only lands if the consulting actually happened first — otherwise it is being overruled with a friendlier label.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Executives play for the company first and their function second.** A CRO argues hard for revenue and still owns the company outcome. Open disagreement with the CFO in the room is healthy; quietly carrying the commercial team's vote into the C-suite is not.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) | flags: needs practitioner check -->

- **Debates that don't belong at C-level go back to the domain owner.** "This doesn't touch revenue — it's the CPO's call." Executive time is for decisions that genuinely cross functions, not for re-opening operational choices someone already owns.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) -->

- **Roles that are built to disagree sit on different people.** The CEO holds compromise and alignment; the CFO holds the line on constraints. Same for whoever owns pricing versus whoever owns volume. Put both mandates in one head and both get softer.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Clear roles plus people you actually trust — structure on its own doesn't work.** What made the early one-room phase fast was a few people who pushed harder on their domain than you would have. At growth you rebuild that deliberately: the right owner per domain, and enough trust to stay out of their call.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

## What you actually need to do

1. **Ask "who did you check with?" until people stop needing the prompt.** Owning a decision means checking cross-effects before calling it — "did you check with the platform owner what this means for them?" If not, it goes back. Most people consult once they know that deciding alone is allowed but deciding unchecked is not.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

2. **Write down, per role, what counts as a valid block.** For the decisions you can't walk back, name what each executive can stop — and what they can't. The CFO's example: a move that would make monthly solvency impossible, which is the baseline the company relies on them to protect. A valid block is reasoned and has to be worked into the solution; "I don't like it" and "I have a better idea" go in as input instead.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

3. **Hand over the role on day one when you hire an executive.** Give real decision rights in their domain and let them run it in their own style. Don't keep them on a leash through months of small shadow projects while you keep the final call.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

4. **Check three things before you hand someone a cross-functional role.** Can they take the authority — skill and maturity? What other hat do they wear, and will they run the role through that one function's lens? Do you trust them on this domain? Then assign it as a mission with an end date — "six-month mission to stand up sales ops" — not as a permanent identity.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

5. **When every team is locally right and the shared outcome still fails, ask who owns the whole thing.** On a multi-sided business — dev, marketing, and monetisation each owning a slice — don't ask one side to write the others' strategy, and don't bolt coordination onto a function lead as an extra hat. Sometimes the answer is a neutral owner sitting above the functions who is accountable for the combined result; practitioners call this "traffic control." It is not required for every company at this size.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

## Warning signs you're behind

### Output quality

- **Leaders negotiate for their function instead of deciding for the company.** An executive defends their area as if carrying a team vote, or a manager keeps everyone locally happy — often from good intentions, and just as common after an internal promotion as after an external hire. Either way the trade-off never gets made at the top, and the negotiation drips down the org as gridlock on cross-team priorities.
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) -->

- **Two mandates that should push against each other sit in one person.** CEO and CFO in one head, or the pricing owner also chasing volume. Both jobs get done at half strength, and you usually find out through a number rather than a conversation.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **Every team is locally right and the shared outcome is still wrong.** Dev, marketing, and monetisation all hit their own goals; the platform result doesn't move. That gap is the signal that nobody owns the outcome across them.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

### Founder / key-person time

- **Your best three people are on every cross-functional project and every business-as-usual fix.** Their time and their "which hat am I wearing" clarity both get squeezed, and there is no clean org-chart answer at this size.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

### Process entry

- **"Careful onboarding" is doing the work of an excuse.** A senior hire drifts through small projects for four months and is then judged slow to start. Usually that is a role that was never handed over, not a bad hire.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **You rolled out a whole framework before the org needed one.** The team spends more time keeping the process alive than moving the business forward. Frameworks like Holacracy hold genuinely useful ideas — blocks that have to be reasoned, decision rights written down — but importing the full rulebook at this stage can be too burdensome. Take the principle and fit it to how your team already works.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

## How this evolves next

- **Org design stays imperfect — manage the squeeze instead of redrawing the chart.** The people good enough for strategic cross-functional work are usually the same people carrying business-as-usual improvements. Name that trade-off out loud each planning cycle rather than hunting for the org chart that removes it.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

- **At scaled, process wraps around founder intuition and structured conflict tools appear.** A shower insight becomes a staged test instead of a company-wide pivot by Monday — see [1.4 at Scaled](1.4--scaled.md).
  <!-- sources: src-064 (anonymous-practitioner) -->
