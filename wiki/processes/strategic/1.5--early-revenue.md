# Organizational Design — Early Revenue (~11–50 people)

> At early revenue, org design is founding-team role growth: give away the jobs you still own, stand up functional owners, and protect CEO-only work — not draw a pretty org chart.

## What good looks like at this stage

- **You build fire departments, not personally put out every fire.** The CEO's job is organisational capacity to handle ops and product fires — not being on-call for every ticket, demo, and "no one's job" task. Cargo-cult hands-off and cargo-cult micromanagement both fail; the target is systems and owners, not a personality rewrite.
  <!-- sources: src-099 (First Round / Corcos, vc_portfolio_interest), src-101 (HN founder-mode, why) -->

- **It is said (YC) that CEO time is reserved for three non-delegable jobs:** hire and coach a senior team so they work well together; write and over-communicate Mission–Strategy–Metrics; co-author authentic culture with early people — not invent values alone in a quiet room.
  <!-- sources: src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

- **A practical success test: you have unscheduled time to think and stay ahead.** A calendar full of other people's work means you are still Phase 1 Doer-in-Chief. Substantial free time to think, drop in, and plan is the clearest sign the leadership team and alignment actually work without you.
  <!-- sources: src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

## What you actually need to do

1. **Make yourself redundant on today's job every day — then hire yourself tomorrow for the next higher-leverage job.** Treat role exit as a continuous practice, not a one-time handoff. If you never create redundancy, every new hire still routes through your old job.
   <!-- sources: src-098 (HN Ask: How/When to Delegate?) -->

2. **Give away your current job every couple of months** if you want to grow as fast as the company. At ~20–25 people, stop coding, demos, support, and menial "no one's job" tasks yourself so you have time for CEO-only work. Replace yourself with people better than you in each major domain — including day-to-day Product.
   <!-- sources: src-099 (Molly Graham via First Round, vc_portfolio_interest), src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

3. **Don't wait until you "feel ready" to lead — run small experiments** where you coach, set outcomes, and stay out of the fire until the identity catches up. Self-worth staked on being the doer who wins makes "stop owning primary processes" feel like identity threat, not a skill upgrade. Act like a leader first; the story follows.
   <!-- sources: src-106 (Ibarra, sells_book), src-103 (Crocker/Wolfe, why) -->

4. **Manage senior people to quarterly and annual outputs — not weekly milestones — and fire underperforming leadership hires faster than feels comfortable.** Expect roughly 25 percent of first executive hires not to work out. Weekly micromanagement recreates Doer-in-Chief one layer up; slow firing costs more credibility than a temporary void.
   <!-- sources: src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

## Warning signs you're behind

### Founder / key-person time

- **You "delegate" urgent-but-unimportant work** and then burn relationship capital on interruptions about tasks you do not care about. Bad first delegations teach you that "delegation doesn't work" and reinforce holding primary processes.
  <!-- sources: src-098 (HN) -->

- **You sincerely agree you should stop duct-taping ops — and still don't.** Agreement without behaviour change is the immunity pattern: look for competing commitments protecting control, status, or fear of looking incompetent before pushing another ops template.
  <!-- sources: src-104 (Kegan & Lahey, sells_book) -->

- **You never replace yourself with leaders better than you** in each major domain — so you stay Doer-in-Chief and slow everyone else down. Hiring peers who still need your hands on every call is fake Phase 2.
  <!-- sources: src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

### Process entry

- **Every departure or leave feels like a crisis.** At scale, people leaving — and the temporary holes that creates — is a *constant*, not an exception. If processes only work when the same few people never quit or take leave, you are still mistaking Whitewater for "a rough quarter."
  <!-- sources: src-110 (practitioner, human:stefanverkerk) -->

## How this evolves next

- **According to YC: A CEO's first job is to build a product users love; the second job is to build a company to maximize the opportunity that the product has surfaced; and the third is to harvest the profits of the core business to invest in transformative new product ideas. Around 20–25 people, Phase 2 company-building becomes the primary CEO job** (through roughly hundreds of people until a road-tested leadership team can run Phase 1 work without you). Phase 1 success — demand outrunning the small team — is the trigger. Without an explicit shift, the behaviour that won Phase 1 becomes the bottleneck.
  <!-- sources: src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

- **Later: shift company-building burden to the leadership team** so the CEO can invest core-business capacity in transformative new products (Phase 3). That only works if Phase 2 hiring, alignment, and culture actually landed. At growth, org design also means living reporting lines, intentional team boundaries, and a middle-management layer — see [1.5 at Growth](1.5--growth.md).
  <!-- sources: src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest), processes.json 1.5 stageFocus -->

## Tools & resources

- **Immunity-to-Change coaching** — evidence-backed option when founders need behaviour change, not only process templates (Markus 2016 study is promising but thin; pair with structural job-giving, don't treat coaching as magic).
  <!-- sources: src-105 (Markus / JABS), src-104 (Kegan & Lahey, sells_book) -->

- **Workplace coaching generally** — meta-analyses find positive effects on performance, learning, and goal-directed self-regulation. Use as support for the identity/self-regulation side; always pair with naming owners and giving away jobs. See [1.4 Who Decides What](1.4--early-revenue.md) for decision rights; [1.2b Shareholder & Investor Reporting](1.2b--early-revenue.md) when the same Doer-in-Chief pattern shows up in the boardroom. Further reading: [What Got You Here Is What's Hurting You Now](../../../blog/what-got-you-here-is-whats-hurting-you-now.html); [YC — Second job of a startup CEO](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo).
