# Company-Level Planning & Goals — Early Revenue (~20–40 people)

> Planning is not strategy. At early revenue you need a short, repeatable priority list with named owners and an explicit not-list — not a twenty-project backlog or a strategy deck disconnected from financials. Imperfect alignment is fine if everyone can see who owns what.

## What good looks like at this phase

<!-- claim-id: c-001 -->
- **Strategy and planning are different disciplines — and they chain together.** Strategy sets direction; planning translates it through a financial projection into an annual view and then quarterly execution. A strategy document without a financial clip is theatre, not a plan.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-471 -->

<!-- claim-id: c-002 -->
- **Planning starts from what you still want to achieve — a concrete future step and where financials need to be.** Year-axis abstraction alone fails teams; the inbound question is "what's the next real milestone, and what numbers must be true when you get there?"
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-472 -->

<!-- claim-id: c-003 -->
- **About three company priorities for the year — not a twenty-project backlog everyone ignores.** The org should be able to repeat the short list; focus fails without an explicit limit on what counts as a company priority.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-476 -->

<!-- claim-id: c-004 -->
- **An explicit not-list / parking lot sits alongside the yes-list.** Naming what you will not do now ("this we do; that we don't") is as valuable as naming priorities — implicit nos create rumour and drift.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-475 -->

<!-- claim-id: c-005 -->
- **Every priority has a named owner; projects are findable in one shared format with a stated plan.** Teams know what others are doing because work is visible — shared format, owners named, plan written down. Duplication and gaps come from invisible work.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-479, src-023-gitlab (GitLab OKRs, 2017) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-006 -->
- **Imperfect alignment across layers is acceptable when ownership is visible.** Lower-level goals do not need to match company priorities perfectly — taxonomy work slows execution. What matters is that each layer can see who owns which part of the outcome.
  <!-- sources: src-023-gitlab (GitLab OKRs, 2017), src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-007 -->
- **Continuous drop-out is treated as structural, not a series of fires.** An org so lean that one sick person collapses the system is mis-designed; plan for absence and chronic capacity gaps as design facts rather than emergency exceptions.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-477 -->

## What you actually need to do

<!-- claim-id: c-008 -->
1. **Start inbound: what do you still want to achieve next, and where must financials be?** Use this diagnostic before picking projects — planning via a concrete future step beats a blank-slate "what should we do this year?" exercise that stalls.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-478 -->

<!-- claim-id: c-009 -->
2. **Pick ~3 things for the year and surface the real ones.** Example pattern: change the pricing model, put a service concept to market, and one more — not fifteen plausible ideas that don't connect.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-479 -->

<!-- claim-id: c-010 -->
3. **Make projects collectively findable — shared format, owners named, stated plan.** Once you're past the one-table phase, founder-team → org communication of "what are we doing and why" must work differently; visibility is the mechanism.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-479 -->

<!-- claim-id: c-011 -->
4. **Say no explicitly — maintain the parking lot / not-list.** Visible nos prevent the org from learning priorities through rumour or from one founder's workstream dominating without a shared narrative.
   <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-480 -->

<!-- claim-id: c-012 -->
5. **Prefix or tag each key result with an owner so every part of a company goal has a visible directly responsible person.** If nobody is named, it is probably not going to happen — ownership makes goals operational, not decorative.
   <!-- sources: src-023-gitlab (GitLab OKRs, 2017) -->

## Warning signs you're behind

### Output quality

<!-- claim-id: c-013 -->
- **Strategy documents exist but don't connect to metrics or an operational plan.** Theatre without an execution clip — the planning chain breaks at the translation step.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-473 -->

<!-- claim-id: c-014 -->
- **Planning relies on frameworks but lacks project-management skill to finish.** Generic ex-consultant frameworks get you through the first phase then break — projects don't complete without delivery discipline.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-474 -->

<!-- claim-id: c-015 -->
- **The rest of the org learns priorities only through rumour.** One founder's funding track or workstream dominates without a shared narrative — nine months of uncertainty for the team.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-482 -->

<!-- claim-id: c-016 -->
- **Teams cannot explain how their work connects to company priorities, or they claim everything is important.** Without a visible cascade or short priority list, each team optimizes locally, not for the company.
  <!-- sources: src-023-gitlab (GitLab OKRs, 2017), src-070 (serial-ceo) -->

### Founder / key-person time

<!-- claim-id: c-017 -->
- **People literally can't say what they want — planning stalls on a blank slate.** Exactly why starting from "what we won't do" and "the three things" works when vision is still fuzzy.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-483 -->

<!-- claim-id: c-018 -->
- **The founder or a single ops person manually reconnects every team goal to company strategy in spreadsheets.** If the cascade or priority list is not explicit and findable, someone has to do the integration work — and it scales poorly.
  <!-- sources: src-023-gitlab (GitLab OKRs, 2017) -->

### Process entry

<!-- claim-id: c-019 -->
- **Projects are not findable or not in one format — nobody knows who owns what.** No shared project system means duplication, gaps, and teams working on invisible parallel tracks.
  <!-- sources: src-070 (serial-ceo), atom-481 -->

## How this evolves next

<!-- claim-id: c-020 -->
- **At ~40+ headcount, add quarterly cadence, resource planning, and a COO-owned planning process.** Early-revenue rituals — three year priorities, findable projects, explicit nos — are insufficient at scale. The CEO owns ambition and story; the COO (or equivalent) owns capacity, priority enforcement, and the planning rhythm. For the full quarterly operating model: [1.1 @ Growth](../../../wiki/processes/strategic/1.1--growth.md).
