# Convincing founders to change — Early Revenue / Whitewater (~11–50 people)

> The organisation needs the founder to stop being Doer-in-Chief. Agreement is not change — identity, competing commitments, and competence traps keep the old mode in place until you give away jobs and protect CEO-only work.

## What good looks like at this phase

<!-- claim-id: c-001 -->
- **You build fire departments, not personally put out every fire.** The CEO's job is organisational capacity to handle ops and product fires — not being on-call for every ticket, demo, and "no one's job" task. Cargo-cult hands-off and cargo-cult micromanagement both fail; the target is systems and owners, not a personality rewrite.
  <!-- sources: src-099 (First Round / Corcos, vc_portfolio_interest), src-101 (HN founder-mode, why) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-002 -->
- **CEO time is reserved for three non-delegable jobs:** hire and coach a senior team so they work well together; write and over-communicate Mission–Strategy–Metrics; co-author authentic culture with early people — not invent values alone in a quiet room.
  <!-- sources: src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-003 -->
- **A practical success test: you have unscheduled time to think and stay ahead.** A calendar full of other people's work means you are still Phase 1 Doer-in-Chief. Substantial free time to think, drop in, and plan is the clearest sign the leadership team and alignment actually work without you.
  <!-- sources: src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

## What you actually need to do

<!-- claim-id: c-004 -->
1. **Make yourself redundant on today's job every day — then hire yourself tomorrow for the next higher-leverage job.** Treat role exit as a continuous practice, not a one-time handoff. If you never create redundancy, every new hire still routes through your old job.
   <!-- sources: src-098 (HN Ask: How/When to Delegate?) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-005 -->
2. **Give away your current job every couple of months** if you want to grow as fast as the company. At ~20–25 people, stop coding, demos, support, and menial "no one's job" tasks yourself so you have time for CEO-only work. Replace yourself with people better than you in each major domain — including day-to-day Product.
   <!-- sources: src-099 (Molly Graham via First Round, vc_portfolio_interest), src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-006 -->
3. **When you keep a decision, write down the criteria and let others poke holes.** That is how they learn to take the next ones. Criteria that live only in the founder's head keep you as sole decider forever.
   <!-- sources: src-098 (HN) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-007 -->
4. **Map competing commitments and big assumptions about letting go before you push another delegation checklist.** Founders can sincerely agree they should stop duct-taping ops and still not change — Immunity to Change is the pattern. Under stress they double down on the strength that won early (competence trap / strengths overused); treating non-follow-through as laziness wastes the advisory relationship.
   <!-- sources: src-104 (Kegan & Lahey, sells_book), src-102 (March, why), src-107 (Kaiser/Kaplan, sells_book, why), src-103 (Crocker/Wolfe, why) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-008 -->
5. **Don't wait until you "feel ready" to lead — run small experiments** where you coach, set outcomes, and stay out of the fire until the identity catches up. Self-worth staked on being the doer who wins makes "stop owning primary processes" feel like identity threat, not a skill upgrade. Act like a leader first; the story follows.
   <!-- sources: src-106 (Ibarra, sells_book), src-103 (Crocker/Wolfe, why) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-009 -->
6. **Manage senior people to quarterly and annual outputs — not weekly milestones — and fire underperforming leadership hires faster than feels comfortable.** Expect roughly a quarter of first executive hires not to work out. Weekly micromanagement recreates Doer-in-Chief one layer up; slow firing costs more credibility than a temporary void.
   <!-- sources: src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-019 -->
7. **Run a quarterly "not-a-board-meeting" checkpoint with investors and advisors, even before you have a formal board.** It's the cheapest way to practice the exact skill a real board will demand of you: synthesizing a complex business into a focused narrative and running a discussion, not a status readout.
   <!-- sources: src-092 (Rishi Taparia / Garuda Ventures, vc_investor_self_interest) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-020 -->
8. **Don't wait for Series A or B to take this discipline seriously.** The communication and relationship-building skill compounds — starting early is worth more than starting formally.
   <!-- sources: src-095 (NFX, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

## Warning signs you're behind

### Founder / key-person time

<!-- claim-id: c-010 -->
- **You "delegate" urgent-but-unimportant work** and then burn relationship capital on interruptions about tasks you do not care about. Bad first delegations teach you that "delegation doesn't work" and reinforce holding primary processes.
  <!-- sources: src-098 (HN) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-011 -->
- **You sincerely agree you should stop duct-taping ops — and still don't.** Agreement without behaviour change is the immunity pattern: look for competing commitments protecting control, status, or fear of looking incompetent before pushing another ops template.
  <!-- sources: src-104 (Kegan & Lahey, sells_book) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-012 -->
- **You never replace yourself with leaders better than you** in each major domain — so you stay Doer-in-Chief and slow everyone else down. Hiring peers who still need your hands on every call is fake Phase 2.
  <!-- sources: src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-022 -->
- **You use board-meeting time to justify your own performance and decisions instead of asking for help.** It's the same Doer-in-Chief defensiveness as c-012, just relocated to the boardroom — and it can quietly eat the majority of the meeting without you noticing.
  <!-- sources: src-095 (NFX, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

### Process entry

<!-- claim-id: c-013 -->
- **You hire a COO or chief of staff because you are overloaded — without a mandate for structure and honest pushback.** Title without behaviour change leaves you overruling ops or dumping undifferentiated work. Change the decision rights first; then staff the role that holds them.
  <!-- sources: src-100 (HN Should You Hire A COO?) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-014 -->
- **Every departure or leave feels like a crisis.** At scale, people leaving — and the temporary holes that creates — is a *constant*, not an exception. If processes only work when the same few people never quit or take leave, you are still mistaking Whitewater for "a rough quarter."
  <!-- sources: src-110 (practitioner, human:stefanverkerk) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-023 -->
- **You're intimidated or star-struck by your board members' reputations in the room.** That's a sign you've forgotten it's still your meeting to run — the inverse of "act like a leader first" (see c-008).
  <!-- sources: src-097 (The Juice HQ) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-024 -->
- **You avoid sharing bad news with your board out of fear of looking incompetent.** By the time they hear about a real problem, it's too late for anyone to actually help — the same competing-commitment pattern as c-011, aimed outward at the board instead of inward at the team.
  <!-- sources: src-091 (David Paul, vc_investor_self_interest) -->

## How this evolves next

<!-- claim-id: c-015 -->
- **Around 20–25 people, Phase 2 company-building becomes the primary CEO job** (through roughly hundreds of people until a road-tested leadership team can run Phase 1 work without you). Phase 1 success — demand outrunning the small team — is the trigger. Without an explicit shift, the behaviour that won Phase 1 becomes the bottleneck.
  <!-- sources: src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-016 -->
- **Later: shift company-building burden to the leadership team** so the CEO can invest core-business capacity in transformative new products (Phase 3). That only works if Phase 2 hiring, alignment, and culture actually landed.
  <!-- sources: src-111 (Rowghani / YC, vc_portfolio_interest) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-021 -->
- **If you already ran informal quarterly checkpoints before your board existed, you'll be dramatically better prepared once a real board is seated at your next round.** The habit — not the formality — is what compounds.
  <!-- sources: src-092 (Rishi Taparia / Garuda Ventures, vc_investor_self_interest) -->

## Tools & resources

<!-- claim-id: c-017 -->
- **Immunity-to-Change coaching** — evidence-backed option when founders need behaviour change, not only process templates (Markus 2016 study is promising but thin; pair with structural job-giving, don't treat coaching as magic).
  <!-- sources: src-105 (Markus / JABS), src-104 (Kegan & Lahey, sells_book) -->

<!-- claim-id: c-018 -->
- **Workplace coaching generally** — meta-analyses find positive effects on performance, learning, and goal-directed self-regulation. Use as support for the identity/self-regulation side; always pair with naming owners and giving away jobs (see also 1.4 Who Decides What).
