# Research annex — The Gap Where Friction Lives

Methodology and sources

This annex documents how the research for *The Gap Where Friction Lives* was assembled: which models were selected, why, and what the empirical evidence base looks like for each. Citations follow APA 7th edition style. URLs are included only where verified against primary sources during the research process; other sources are cited fully so they can be located via Google Scholar or a DOI resolver.

**Provenance note (2026-09):** This annex was written in March 2026 by a prior research pass (separate LLM session). It is preserved here as the methodology record. The registered pipeline sources/atoms for claims that made the post are in `source-map.md` (`src-083`–`086`, `atom-626`–`639`). Broader lifecycle, empirics, and identity/change layers below were researched but not yet atomised or integrated into the post.

## Starting point: two models already in the operator vocabulary

The research started from the working vocabulary of an operator and consultant. Two frameworks were already in play from conversations with founders and fellow operators: Greiner's phases-and-crises model of organisational growth (Greiner, 1972, 1998), and Rachel Turner's leadership-mode framework for founders (Turner, 2022). These two describe the same journey from different sides of the room — Greiner from the organisational structure, Turner from the founder's leadership style — and comparing them side by side already surfaced the pattern that a crisis at the organisational level maps onto an identity shift at the personal level.

## Searching for a third angle

We then looked systematically for a third lens that would add something the first two lacked. The candidates considered were:

- Adizes' Corporate Lifecycles, together with the PAEI role model (Adizes, 1988, 1999).
- Les McKeown's Predictable Success (McKeown, 2010).
- Noam Wasserman's The Founder's Dilemmas (Wasserman, 2012).
- Jim Collins' Level 5 leadership from Good to Great (Collins, 2001).
- Charan, Drotter, and Noel's The Leadership Pipeline (Charan, Drotter, & Noel, 2001).

Wasserman, Collins, and Charan were set aside because they are founder- or leader-centric lenses that reinforce Turner without adding an organisational dimension. Adizes and McKeown were retained because they name the specific transitional phase where operational consulting typically enters — Adizes' Adolescence and McKeown's Whitewater.

## Selecting McKeown, borrowing PAEI from Adizes

McKeown was selected as the primary third lens for actionability: fewer stages, immediate recognition of "Whitewater" by founders, and a team-role typology (Visionary, Operator, Processor, Synergist) that legitimises the fractional COO / operations-lead role directly. Adizes' PAEI role model (Producer, Administrator, Entrepreneur, Integrator) was borrowed as an explanatory device — specifically to explain why the transition is resisted: founders in the early phases are typically strong on Entrepreneur (E) and Producer (P) functions, and the transition demands Administrator (A), which is neither where their identity lives nor what earned them their early success.

## Broader lifecycle literature considered

To validate that these three models are not an idiosyncratic selection, a broader survey of organisational lifecycle models was conducted. Foundational contributions include:

- Lippitt and Schmidt (1967) on crises in developing organisations.
- Galbraith (1982) on stages of growth in high-technology organisations.
- Churchill and Lewis (1983) on the five stages of small business growth.
- Quinn and Cameron (1983), who synthesised nine earlier stage models into a four-phase meta-framework and linked stages to organisational effectiveness criteria.
- Miller and Friesen (1984), one of the few genuinely longitudinal empirical validations of a lifecycle model.
- Scott and Bruce (1987) on the stages of small business growth.
- Hanks, Watson, Jansen, and Chandler (1993), who used cluster analysis on high-technology firms to identify stage configurations empirically.

The models chosen for the post are representative of the field rather than the only defensible options. The choice was made on communicative fit for founders rather than empirical dominance.

## Empirical evidence for the stage models themselves

The empirical evidence for stage-model claims is mixed to weak. Some studies find validation — Miller and Friesen (1984); Quinn and Cameron (1983); and more recent case-based support such as the six-firm study of Greiner's model in Czech automotive companies by Sukova (2020). Others find that organisational evolution does not follow the deterministic patterns the models predict (Tushman, Newman, & Romanelli, 1986).

Adizes' work in particular has been widely applied by practitioners but rarely tested independently. McKeown similarly has practitioner traction but no independent peer-reviewed validation. Turner's book is a practitioner text without empirical study.

Where the empirical evidence is stronger is on specific components rather than on the full stage sequences. Greiner's "leadership crisis" in particular has been supported by recent large-N work. Lee and Kim (2024), published in the *Strategic Management Journal*, use a dataset of 6.3 million job postings from more than 38,000 US startups founded after 2010 to measure when startups begin scaling, and cite Greiner's (1972, p. 41) "crisis of leadership" as the theoretical anchor for the administrative complexity that emerges as ventures grow beyond the founding team's capacity. The takeaway framed in the research: the phase models function best as shared language and diagnostic compass, not as deterministic predictors.

## The behavioural and identity layer

Because the post's central thesis — that friction is the gap between what the organisation needs and what the founder is doing — is inherently a behavioural claim, a second research layer was assembled around identity and change. Core references:

- March (1991) on the competence trap: organisations and individuals continue to exploit what already works, undermining the exploration needed to adapt.
- Kaiser and Kaplan (2009) on strengths overused becoming weaknesses, based on their large-sample work on the Leadership Versatility Index.
- Kegan (1994) on subject-object theory (adult development), and Kegan and Lahey (2009) on Immunity to Change.
- Ibarra (2003, 2015) on identity-based leadership transitions, based on longitudinal studies of leaders changing role.
- Crocker and Wolfe (2001) on contingencies of self-worth, providing a psychological account of why status-linked identities are difficult to relinquish.

For interventions:

- Coaching effectiveness has moderate-to-strong meta-analytic support. Theeboom, Beersma, and van Vianen (2014) meta-analysed 18 studies covering roughly 2,500 participants and found consistent positive effects across performance/skills, well-being, coping, work attitudes, and goal-directed self-regulation. Jones, Woods, and Guillaume (2016) similarly found positive effects on learning and performance outcomes across workplace coaching studies.
- Immunity to Change specifically has one substantive quantitative study: Markus (2016), published in the *Journal of Applied Behavioral Science*, compared 45 supervisors receiving ITC coaching against a 25-supervisor control group and found the coached group reported significantly more progress toward their leadership development goals than the comparison group.

## What made it into the post, and what didn't

The published version of *The Gap Where Friction Lives* uses Greiner, Turner, and McKeown as its three lenses, with PAEI as the explanatory device for why founders resist the transition. It maps these onto the operational maturity stages of the Duct Tape to COO diagnostic. The broader lifecycle literature, the empirical evidence discussion, and the identity/change layer are researched but not yet integrated — they were held as material for a separate, more empirical follow-up post rather than crowding the first piece.
