## Summary

First-person, self-critical essay by a working VC on how investor pressure on founders actually functions, and how he changed his own behavior. He describes his own earlier pattern of confrontational board behavior toward underperforming founders — publicly pressuring and shaming founders who missed numbers — and explains why he abandoned it: it damages the trust that lets him hear about problems early, which is the actual mechanism boards need to be useful. Explicitly names the cascade that produces the "more control" reflex: investors face pressure from their own LPs to explain portfolio underperformance, and that pressure gets passed down to founders as tightened scrutiny, which is a natural but often counterproductive reflex. Companion piece to an earlier essay "Communicating with Founders is like Handling Cats." Bias flagged as VC self-interest, but the essay argues against the author's own past behavior, which is evidence against pure self-serving framing.

## Notable claims

- The author used to publicly pressure and shame founders for missing numbers in board meetings, and now considers that counterproductive to maintaining trust
- If founders become afraid to share bad news, investors find out about real problems too late to help — fear-based board dynamics degrade the information a board needs to do its job
- Investor pressure toward founders often originates one level up: investors are accountable to their own LPs for portfolio performance, and that accountability pressure cascades down onto founders as tightened scrutiny
- Knowing when to apply pressure and when to ease off is a discernible skill, not a fixed investor style — the same investor can do both depending on the situation
