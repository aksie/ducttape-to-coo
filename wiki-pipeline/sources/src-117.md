## Summary

Dutch practitioner interview, 6 October 2026. Stefan Verkerk interviewing. The contributor sat on a three-founder board and also ran branches in a consultancy that grew from roughly 7–10 people to a peak near 500, using a cell model from about 40. Names of people, the company, clients and places are anonymised in the structured contribution. The raw transcript stays in the private team repo.

The experience is first-hand and from a board seat, in one people business. Commercial claims are strongest for complex services, which is why the sales sections (5.1, 5.2, 5.4) were left for a later pass. What was extracted is how the cells were designed, how the founder board decided, what happened when pay was pushed to peers, and how cash broke when hiring ran ahead of billing.

## Notable claims

- A cell model around 40 people kept a small-company feel near 500, and the point of autonomy was cloning the model, not local invention.
- Staff functions were refused. Leaders were all-rounders. Standard finance, HR admin and legal were outsourced.
- Two career paths were named at entry: all-round leadership, or a well-paid specialist path that does not enter the management team.
- A branch that abandoned the model for secondment was the first place culture slid, and it was closed.
- Equal founders chose no CEO. Consensus was the rule, with a bounded pause when one person felt something was wrong but could not yet say why.
- The board never gave itself the feedback it required of branch directors. Felt equality rested on a majority owner choosing to act that way.
- Peer token votes for raises flattened pay and could leave directors with nothing. Many people said pay was management's job.
- An in-house academy in the first months, taught mostly by leaders, was half craft and half working with people.
- Keeping a top performer who broke the culture, because that cell's numbers held the rest up, was the miss they would not repeat.
- Hire–train–bill took longer than client payment terms. Nobody formally owned the cash plan until payroll was at risk.
