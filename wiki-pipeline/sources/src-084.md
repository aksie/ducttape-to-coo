## Summary

Long-form essay by a career VC (now academic) arguing that founders are systematically told a rosier story about board dynamics than reality supports. Central argument: the choice to stop being CEO is presented to founders as if it will always be their own decision, but in practice it is the board's decision far more often than founders are led to believe, and by the time a founder realizes this, the board has already lost confidence. The post inspired a book co-authored with Elizabeth Zalman ("Founder vs Investor") with a dedicated chapter on how boards actually operate. Author's VC background is a bias signal, but the essay argues explicitly against the pro-board, pro-VC narrative, which cuts against simple commercial self-interest — treated as high-credibility practitioner content precisely because it is adversarial to the dominant "boards are here to help you" framing.

## Notable claims

- Founders are told the CEO transition will be their call; in practice, the board makes that call far more often than founders expect
- Three realistic CEO-transition outcomes, from best to worst: founder pushes for their own succession and it's accepted, founder is told to resign or be fired, founder is removed outright — the first is uncommon
- The gap between "board advice" as commonly written and how boards actually behave under pressure is large enough that founders are routinely blindsided
- Founders who assume good intent and informal trust are the load-bearing structure of the relationship are relying on something the formal power structure does not actually guarantee
