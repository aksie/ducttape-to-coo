# 5.1 Sales Operations / 5.2 Customer Acquisition

## First Hires → Early Revenue (~10–40 · founder- and expert-led sales)

### What good looks like

- **Experts sell, not sales people** — consultants (whose main job is advising and billing hours) are put in front of prospects early. In complex services sales a subject expert at the table builds far more trust than a smooth salesperson who has to say "I'll need to ask a colleague" after three sentences.
- **Commercial skills are trained deliberately** — heavy investment in commercial skills for consultants; people who grew up in the company's culture understood why there were no external sales hires and bought into it.
- **Founders' role is getting experts to the table** — once experts are in the room "they're killers"; the bottleneck is network and first conversations. Founders helped with qualifying and closing early on, because experts chase everything ("everything is interesting").
- **Content events are the acquisition engine** — cold calling and calling campaigns did not work. What worked: organising (or co-hosting) content events — event logistics can be outsourced — and letting your experts walk around and speak on stage. People approach them on their expertise; the company only has to *facilitate the conversation happening*. Size of the event mattered little.
- **CRM from very early** — interviewee is surprised how late people-business companies adopt CRM; they had it "very fast".

### What you actually need to do

1. **Put experts in front of prospects** in complex sales; train them in commercial skills from the start.
2. **Run content events instead of cold outreach** — outsource logistics, put your people on stage.
3. **Set up a simple CRM early** — one that supports their way of working, not an administrative dragon.
4. **Founders own the first meeting and help qualify** until experts can do it themselves.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Hardcore specialists forced into selling** — for deeply introverted engineers selling drains energy for hours afterwards; select "sheep with five legs" who can do consultancy *and* talk to clients.
- **Experts chasing every lead** — no qualification, too many pursuits.

### How this evolves next

- **The resistance phase arrives** — see Growth.

## Growth → Scaled (~40–450 · branch / cell model)

### What good looks like

- **Commercial responsibility stays with the people who deliver** — "everyone finds sales dirty, scary and uncomfortable", so explaining that a consultant role carries commercial responsibility creates resistance. Put energy into dissolving it: make it playful, build skills around it, give a CRM that *supports* rather than administers. Very simple processes that match their approach.
- **Sales management sits with the person primarily responsible for growth** — in the cell model that is the branch director: responsible for growth and success, and commerce falls under that. Supported by dashboards (close rate, averages) rather than a dedicated sales-analysis hire.
- **All business is new business** — no rules about what counts as new vs existing business. Everyone gets one revenue target; growing existing accounts counts.
- **If you split a target, split the amount, not the type** — at branch level several people can each own part of the revenue number, but the goal type stays the same: euros, revenue growth for this branch. (Contrast with Serial CEO's P-vs-Q split — interviewee sees no conflict: he splits *who brings how much*, not *kinds of business*.)
- **Duo formation (master–apprentice)** — every commercially responsible person has a content expert linked to them; over time that expert steps into commerce and takes on their own content person.
- **Honesty in the sales role** — coaching advice to a salesperson: don't play a role you don't have; tell clients openly you're sales and take them seriously — they see through a game.
- **Be willing to fire clients for your people** — twice (including large clients) the company told clients "we're not continuing, our people don't become happy working for you." Clients had never experienced that.

### What you actually need to do

1. **Install a very simple qualification process** — what do we pursue, what don't we. (First thing he fixes wherever he comes in: experts like every contact and want to please.)
2. **Form commercial–content duos** and make the content person grow into commerce.
3. **Give everyone one revenue goal** — no new/existing split, no lead-gen/follow-up split.
4. **Do real sales management** — "good sales management is a craft": analyse funnels, where business comes from, which accounts grew and which didn't, where energy goes.
5. **Make responsibilities explicit** — clarity about who is responsible for what.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Specialisation creep** — first one MT member becomes delivery manager and is exempted from commercial targets; the rest feel they work harder. Later lead generation is split from follow-up: in complex product/services sales that goes from **zero to three handover moments per sale** — "killing". Fix on return: give everyone primary responsibility again.
- **Resistance wins → specialists hired** — consultants stop filling the sales role, say "get specialists for it", sales people get hired, "and then everything goes wrong".
- **Energy spent on internal negotiation** — endless debates whether a deal is new or existing business (e.g. client bought before, but two years ago), driven by sub-targets (no bonuses involved).
- **Living off existing accounts** — teams can succeed for a few quarters, even 1–2 years, on existing accounts while avoiding cold outreach; it stops eventually. You can't sustain a commercial process without new business.
- **"Who generated vs who followed up" blame** — "great lead, you followed it up badly" is energy lost; never force it.

### How this evolves next

- Interviewee reports the post-departure company drifted further toward specialised sales roles and had to be repaired toward primary responsibility again.

# 2.1 Cash Flow Management (partial)

## Early Revenue → Growth

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Hire–train–bill lag longer than payment terms** — people are hired, trained ~2 months, billable after ~4 months; the investment-to-return gap is longer than client payment terms. Scaling fast without watching this means hitting the brakes.
- **Nobody formally owns financial planning** — sat informally with one founder; taken seriously only when payroll was at risk and the credit facility no longer sufficed.

### What good looks like / evolution

- **After a near-miss, remove bank dependency** — the bank reassured them within fifteen minutes (it could see what they generated), but the founders decided "we never want this feeling again" and wound down dependency on banks for access to their own funds. Financial ownership was formalised afterwards.

# 1.5 Organizational Design

## Growth (~40 → scaled)

### What good looks like

- **Cell model introduced around ~40 people** — the founders all came from large hierarchical corporates and didn't want that. Inspired by a book on cell-based organisations ("it describes mainly the nice parts"). First split was simple: one branch became two and the existing directors were divided over the two cells; every later split got harder.
- **Cells keep the small-company feel at 500** — small autonomous businesses, so it never feels like working for a big company, except at big company parties.
- **No staff functions** — "staff functions were strictly forbidden" (people business, overhead). Functional areas live in the MT: *field management* — each MT member responsible for a number of consultants' wellbeing.
- **Outsource standardised processes early** — payroll and simple admin with an office manager at first; as they grew, standardised processes (finance, HR admin, legal) were outsourced immediately.
- **All-round leadership roles** — leadership includes commerce, delivery and people (getting people to work, keeping clients happy); finance/HR/legal are outsourced. Downside: the roles are heavy.
- **Explicit two career paths** — make clear at entry: the leadership path requires being an all-rounder; becoming a hardcore IT architect is a valid, well-paid specialist path but does not lead into the MT.
- **The cell model is about cloning** — autonomy does not mean doing whatever you like.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **A cell drifts from the model and nobody corrects it** — one branch decided project work with juniors "doesn't work in our business", hired older staff for secondment instead; culture slid there first; that branch never made it and was closed. "We should have guarded more tightly that we clone."
- **Profit skimming inside a cell** — branch norm was ~10% margin ("then you don't hear from the board"); one director made ~20% and spent the surplus on lavish parties. Great team, but not the intent: surplus should help a struggling sister branch recover. Not made clear enough.
- **Killing an investing director too soon** — a new director at a weak branch gave everyone large development budgets (team trips abroad to scout technology, survival trips); margin dipped below 10% and he was removed too fast in the interviewee's view — "sometimes you need to invest before the return."
- **Managers promoted to branch director too fast** — founders had to step back into running branches.

# 1.4 Who Decides What

## Growth (founder board)

### What good looks like

- **No CEO among equal founders (their choice)** — a proposal to appoint one founder as CEO was reverted after strong objection: hierarchy didn't fit what they were building.
- **Consensus with a bounded emotional veto** — rule: in principle all three agree; majority vote exists, but if one person feels strongly something is wrong — even without words for it yet — it isn't pushed through.
- **Delay must earn its keep** — when someone blocks without arguments (compare: a CTO repeatedly postponing a pricing decision), ask: *what does delay give us?* A week to get arguments in order is valid; after that, trust each other's expertise.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **No mutual feedback or coaching in the board** — offsites (once or twice a year abroad) aligned on big topics, but board members never gave each other hard feedback or coaching; things simmered. Branch directors noticed the inequality: they received feedback and assessments, the board didn't.
- **Felt equality vs real ownership** — one founder held a large majority stake; the felt equality worked only as long as that founder chose to act democratically.
- **Branch autonomy without correction** — each founder also ran a branch; the vision was "lived differently" there and never corrected.

# Culture & leadership example (map to 1.3 / 1.5 / 3.6 — no dedicated cell)

### What good looks like

- **Culture eats strategy** — "with all the strategies and beautiful plans we made, it was our culture that let us win."
- **Codify the culture** — interviewee wrote a booklet on it with the board and pushed it into everything: hiring for all-rounders, academy, celebrations.
- **Push decisions back to values** — when people ask what to do: "you know it yourself, I trust you — go back to what makes our people happy." Successors on the board still use that as the central argument in decisions.
- **Pride as a culture driver** — profile the company bigger than it is (brand awareness *and* pride); celebrate a lot; big company parties.
- **Board example behaviour is what counts** — "what we said didn't matter, how we behaved did"; hierarchy works through example. Requires "military discipline" from board members; if you can't sustain it, you should leave.
- **Leadership is providing clarity** — so professionals can focus on what they're good at and enjoy; that makes management partly a "shit job": giving no raise, bad reviews — don't push that onto employees.
- **What he'd do again (also learnt in education):** make a vision, select people who feel it, let those who don't go elsewhere, give everyone clear goals, clear frames, keep things simple and transparent.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **A board member stops carrying the culture** — the largest shareholder grew more distant from the young workforce and "sat in a golden cage"; when board members don't model it (e.g. telling branches to sell projects while their own branch only did secondment), it trickles through.
- **Visible slack from the board** — generous opportunities (e.g. a funded months-long scouting trip abroad that produced nothing) — interviewee stands by giving chances, but many expected the board to be stricter; visible slack gets copied.
- **Tolerating someone who says out loud they don't believe in the mission** — demotivating for others; correct immediately.

# 3.5 Performance Management (pay)

## Growth

### What good looks like / experiments

- **Salary decisions are where injustice is felt** — top-down raises create a sense of unfairness, so they experimented with self-determined pay. Interviewee still thinks they gave up too fast.
- **Branch-level freedom** — "if your branch makes 10% margin, sort it out yourselves" — but some specialisms rose faster than others and needed correcting.
- **Later model (after he left):** the employee writes their own raise proposal, seeks backup from a colleague, discusses it with the branch director.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Peer token systems level everything** — each branch got a raise budget; everyone distributed five tokens to colleagues (also to themselves). Strong levelling effect: managers were seen as "earning enough", hard-working consultants were favoured; one branch gave its two directors no raise at all, which hit hard.
- **The process makes pay the topic** — attention on the process meant attention on salary, "exactly what you don't want".
- **Employees don't want to own it** — a large group said: autonomy is nice, but this is management's responsibility — "that's what you're there for."

# 3.6 Employee Satisfaction & Feedback

## Growth → Scaled

### What good looks like

- **Choose an instrument that fits your philosophy** — after deciding work happiness was central, they picked Great Place to Work (closest in spirit: positive, pride-oriented; plus a sector variant [?]).
- **Report scores per branch** — results were predictable, but they gave a basis for the conversation with each branch.
- **A visible ambition** — goal to land in the national top 3 each time.

# 3.7 Learning & Development

## Growth

### What good looks like

- **In-house academy for every new hire** — first two months: Young Professional Academy, half technical skills, half people skills.
- **Teach it yourselves** — most training given by founders/leaders to stay close to people and scout talent; only deep technical content from outside.

# 3.2 / 3.4 Hiring & Offboarding — culture underminers

## Growth → Scaled

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Keeping a top performer who breaks the culture** — an overperformer (strong in commerce and client satisfaction) was given a branch; under pressure he stopped working from the core values (trust, professional autonomy), split the cell model his own way, people dropped out, absenteeism rose, it became unsafe. The board kept debating "but he performs" vs "he is breaking our culture" and didn't act. "In a people business you end up breaking more people."
- **The 'cork' argument** — with many cells there are always strong and weak ones; the strong ones keep you afloat, so you protect them. "We shouldn't have done that."
