**Cross-cutting spine (extract as `why` atoms on every 1.4 cell):** Accountability without authority is the root failure. If someone is responsible for an outcome, they must be able to decide on it. Holacracy and PRINCE2 are cited as cautionary tales — the principles are sound, but full systems become theatre, too process-heavy; ~90% of people would rather absorb the principle and apply it in their own style than follow the rulebook.

**Second cross-cutting theme:** Decouple roles from people / identity (“locative” thinking) — missions not titles, interim roles, accept that org design revisits every few months.

# 1.4 Who Decides What

## First Hires (~1–8 · one room)

### What good looks like

- **Founders / a small handful decide everything together** — on a multi-sided platform, content, monetization, and marketing sit at one table, constantly talking, making micro-trade-offs in real time.
- **It works because they’re in one room** — and because they’re self-starting people who deal well with ambiguity.
- **The coordinating function is invisible** — at 3–4 people per side, they *are* their own traffic control; nobody notices it as a distinct role.

### What you actually need to do

1. **Don’t over-formalise** — at this size there’s nothing to fix structurally.
2. **Hire people who can operate when things are vague** — premature process is the failure mode, not missing process.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Premature RACI, holacracy, or project methodology** — fixing structure before the room stops working.

### How this evolves next

- **The inflection point is “it no longer fits in one room.”** The thing that quietly disappears is the coordinating function — you only notice once teams split apart.

## Early Revenue (~20–40 sized team)

### What good looks like

- **Roles carry genuine decision authority, not just task ownership** — the owner can say: “I hear you don’t want this as developers, but the other side weighs heavier, so I’m deciding to go left. I disagree with you, but it’s my responsibility and my authority.” That does **not** absolve them of thinking about the shared goal or explaining the why.
- **Accountability and authority sit with the same person or team** — written down once you’re past the one-room phase.
- **Cross-functional teams for strategic change; functional ownership for BAU** — not permanent matrix for everything. Budget may force you to stay functional longer (can’t afford a full product team yet) — scale and money dictate how much you can split.
- **Type 1 decisions** (reversible, course-correctable) sit with the domain owner — they listen, then decide in their style. A €30k spend that only affects one team and is reversible can still be made fast and alone.
- **Hiring manager owns the hire** — culture / potential / role-fit inputs are scores and advice, not veto.

### What you actually need to do

1. **When you put something on paper, name who decides** — “you go over this” — and mean it.
2. **Before assigning a role, check two things:** (1) Can this person actually take the authority (maturity, skill, task-maturity)? Giving responsibility to a junior who lacks the skills sets up guaranteed failure blamed on “the system.” (2) What other hat are they wearing? If someone always shows up as “the marketing person” and you add a cross-cutting role, they’ll exercise it through the marketing lens — and be seen that way even if they don’t intend to.
3. **Pull cross-functional cells for strategic projects** — clear goal, one lead accountable for delivery, piped roles, people who trust each other’s domain.
4. **Split conflicting hats** — pricing (P) vs volume/growth marketing (Q) should not be one person if that breaks incentives; they must talk well, not merge.
5. **Assign “missions” not permanent identity** — interim VP marketing, “your mission is to stand up content ops for six months” — makes change management easier than title = identity.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Constant “we don’t work cross-functionally anymore” lament on team days** — everyone agrees, nothing changes. The fix people reach for (sprints, a cross-functional project) “magically works” only because time pressure forces collaboration, not because structure changed.
- **“But it used to work / why doesn’t it work now?”** — signal that a coordinating role has gone missing as teams split. Developer, marketing, and monetization become separate large teams; changes that must land in one coordinated move (e.g. in-game purchases need suitable games, willing players, and ad setup) fall between the cracks.
- **Asking one function to write a cross-cutting strategy** — e.g. asking the developer team to write the content strategy — they don’t own the other sides, so it won’t land.
- **Junior people given “self-management” responsibility without skills** — system blamed when it was maturity mismatch.
- **Founder still has effective veto on hires** after naming a hiring manager — recruiters and candidates lost in unclear ownership.
- **Former founder/exec still approves spend without context** — blocks the real owner from learning the role.

### How this evolves next

- **At ~40+** you need a standing “traffic control” role above the functions — don’t bolt coordination onto one of the existing three sides; the hat problem makes that fail fundamentally.

## Growth (~40–70 sized team)

### What good looks like

- **The magic people chase (“but it used to be better!”) is recreated deliberately** — not because the past was better, but because back then you had the right people in the room whom you trusted on their domain. Real ingredients: a **technical/structural layer** (clear roles, clear decision rights) plus a **maturity & trust layer** — people mature enough to hold responsibility, whom you genuinely respect, who’ll push harder on their domain than you would. Example pattern: a data engineer owns revenue attribution end-to-end (interviewer feels zero need to do their work); a legal lead is trusted to say stronger things on legal than the CEO could.
- **A neutral coordinator owns the cross-cutting platform outcome** — not “ask dev team to write content strategy” when they don’t own monetization + audience + developers together.
- **Type 1 vs Type 2 decisions are explicit** — two-system model (fast/autocratic vs slow/integrative); nod to Kahneman fast/slow thinking without treating it as a framework religion.
  - **Type 1 / reversible:** delegate to the role-owner within their authority — benevolent dictator who listens well, decides, and is held accountable.
  - **Type 2 / irreversible & cross-cutting** (merger, sale, big strategic bet, multi-year licensing): integrative process where a role-owner can block — but only under defined conditions with a **reasoned objection** (“I don’t like it” isn’t allowed). Example: CFO whose mandate is solvency can object that a multi-year licensing commitment makes their role impossible. The objection must be **integrated into the solution**, not necessarily adopted wholesale. Holacracy’s integration/objection principle without the theatre.
- **Prefer “I’ve been heard” over Amazon-style “disagree and commit”** — practitioner finds pure disagree-and-commit too harsh. The chair/CEO can say: “I hear you no longer agree, but I’m deciding we do it — I now want your commitment to execute.”
- **CEO and CFO (or comparable) roles are not combined** — CEO seeks compromise and holds things together; CFO holds the hard line on runway/constraints. Same with P vs Q — the one who must do pricing shouldn’t do volume marketing.
- **Decision rights mean “run with the ball”** — new VP gets the role fully from day one (in their style), not four months of shadow project hopping.
- **Roles decoupled from people / identity** — interim VP titles, mission framing (“you’re not VP Marketing, your mission is to stand up a content team”), accept roles are temporary — “we’ll do it this way now and re-do it differently in four months.”
- **Cross-functional works best when people want little of each other’s roles** — no “I’m the senior designer so I should also own that other thing” turf fights.

### What you actually need to do

1. **Add the missing “traffic control” function** when three teams each have valid local logic but the platform outcome fails — authority + accountability for the combined outcome, not branded to one side.
2. **Before giving someone a cross-cutting role, ask:** Can they do it? What other hat do they wear? Do we trust them on this domain?
3. **Document what qualifies as a Type 2 objection** — e.g. CFO: action makes monthly solvency impossible; not preference, not “better idea.”
4. **Coach that owning a decision means checking cross-effects first** — “Did you check with the platform owner what this means?” If no, back to the drawing board — and you’re a bit off for the role. People panic that “now he’ll decide alone”; in practice it almost never happens without consultation.
5. **On executive hire: hand over the role** — “You decide; if you ignore input in month one that’s a qualification issue, not ‘go hobby on side projects.’”

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Two structurally opposed roles collapsed into one person** — CEO + CFO in one head; pricing owner also owns volume marketing.
- **“Careful onboarding” as a euphemism** — new senior hire drifts through small projects for four months, then judged “slow to start” — failure of willingness to hand over the role, not proof you hired wrong.
- **Teams argue from “how we did it when we were four people”** — nostalgia for the small room.
- **Holacracy / heavy process theater** — people operate the system, not the business; ~90% resent rule-keeping.
- **Senior functional leads fight over ownership** — tribes (mobile vs web) never align.
- **Every hire is a culture-match drama with implicit founder veto.**

### How this evolves next

- **Capacity squeeze with no clean answer** — the people good enough to run cross-functional strategic projects are usually the same people who carry strategic optimisation for BAU. Time squeeze + “which hat” squeeze on your best people. Manage by accepting permanent imperfection, not one perfect org chart.
- **Scaled (51+):** more formal integration paths for Type 2 decisions; standing planning/COO function owns capacity “no” — see 1.1 growth.

# 1.1 Strategy & Planning (planning cycle · not strategy craft)

## Cross-stage principles

### What good looks like

- **Strategy and planning are different things** — and the chain breaks between them. Strategy → translation-to-strategy (how I get there) → financial plan → annual → quarterly. A strategy isn’t complete without a financial projection hung on it.
- **Strategy-making is conceptually hard and rare** — most founders think they can do it; very few people actually can. Demands data & metrics maturity: a few good metrics (not the many bad ones you’ll be tempted by), then clip operational planning onto them and define what “success this year” means.
- **Planning starts from “what do you still want to achieve?”** — a future step (financials, the improvement step), not a year-axis abstraction alone.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Strategy documents exist but don’t connect to metrics or operational plan** — strategy craft without metrics maturity is mostly theatre.
- **Generic ex-consultants who love a framework but lack real project-management skill** — content-strong + goal-oriented people get you through the first phase but it eventually breaks.

## Early Revenue (~20–40 sized team)

### What good looks like

- **Even naming what you will not do is valuable** — e.g. “we’ll scale on home-visit X now, we have funding for that; the company-finding add-on we will not do yet.” That’s planning, not strategy — this we do now, that we don’t.
- **A short priority list the org can repeat** — ~three company priorities this year (practitioner cites an external operator: “three must-do things per year”); not a twenty-project backlog everyone ignores.
- **A visible not-list / parking lot** — as valuable as the yes-list.
- **Rhythm, capacity/resourcing, and communicating it to the org** — once you’re past one-table, founder-team → org communication of “what are we doing and why” must work very differently, and usually doesn’t at first.
- **Accept continuous drop-out as structural** — an org so lean it collapses when one person is sick / on leave / troubleshooting is mis-designed; treat chronic capacity gaps as design fact, not a series of fires.

### What you actually need to do

1. **Start inbound diagnostic:** what do you still want to achieve next — concrete step in future; where do financials need to be?
2. **Pick ~3 things for the year** — surface the few real ones (“this year we change the pricing model, put this service concept to market, and one more”).
3. **Make projects collectively findable** — shared format, owners named, stated plan; teams know what others are doing.
4. **Say no explicitly** — parking lot / not-list.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Projects not findable or not in one format** — nobody knows who owns what.
- **Rest of org learns priorities only through rumour** — one founder’s funding track dominates without shared narrative.
- **People literally can’t say what they want** — exactly why starting from “what we won’t do / the three things” works.

### How this evolves next

- **At ~40+:** quarterly cadence, resource planning, COO-owned process — see growth section.

## Growth (~40–70 sized team) · primary depth

### What good looks like

- **Concrete quarterly rhythm:**
  - **~3 weeks before team day / planning cycle:** revisit strategy, year’s objectives, set this quarter’s business + strategic priorities and projects within them.
  - **3–5 projects max for the quarter** (depending on team size), each **fully staffed/resourced** — resource planning is the step that, if skipped, breaks everything immediately.
  - **Mid-quarter check** at ~5–6 weeks (sometimes a light whole-company “business review” variant; monthly check-ins also possible).
  - **Weekly management-team review that is issue-based** — each week, just hear: update / movement / bottleneck? Don’t discuss what’s running fine. Stoplight dashboards explicitly judged not to work; an informal weekly mental walk-through of every project worked better.
- **Quarterly company roadmap synced with product roadmap** — not 100% overlap (BAU, HR cycles, events, fundraising sit outside product) but same top priorities and capacity reality.
- **Big efforts as multi-quarter projects with phases** — not a new disposable project each quarter. “It’s a quarterly project; we’re now entering the next phase.” Three margin-improvement projects across three quarters that sum to the goal. You can’t lock all three a year ahead (that fails), but you should know the steps and pick the next one — “step 2 didn’t land, so step 2 runs another quarter.”
- **COO (or equivalent) owns the planning process** — capacity, priority enforcement, templates, “no”; sits closer to CFO than CEO. **CEO owns ambition and story**, not being the process police or instigating daily reprioritization.
- **Cross-functional staffing for strategic projects; functional org for BAU optimisation** — same people often both → explicit squeeze management.

### What you actually need to do

1. **Quarterly:** pick strategic projects, assign owners, staff them (or admit you can’t).
2. **Weekly leadership:** walk strategic projects — only deep-dive where stuck; skip green.
3. **Keep product roadmap coupled to company priorities but not identical** — scoping a big new feature can itself be a 1–2 month project with business stakeholders; roadmap must reflect sales/funding/performance cycles outside product.
4. **Calibrate detail to maturity** — still-vague project shouldn’t be forced into a template; well-understood one should be.
5. **When a bet really is big enough to disrupt the plan:** role-owner argues “this is huge, I need a lot of capacity”; CEO weighs it — CEO cannot be the instigator of constant “wait, let’s do something else.”

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Quarterly-only review** — too late; projects drift six weeks before anyone notices.
- **Quarterly planning without resource planning** — fails immediately.
- **Twenty “priority” projects** — no execution discipline.
- **Thinking each quarter means throwing the previous project away** — instead of advancing its next phase.
- **Product roadmap = company roadmap** — sales, platform, GTM, HR cycles invisible until crisis.

### How this evolves next

- **Larger orgs:** longer project horizons, same rhythm scaled; dedicated planning/COO function non-optional.

### Tools & resources

- Issue-based project review in weekly MT (not RAG theatre for everything).
- External priority engagement (practitioner): expensive outside help + fixed calendar rhythm + high potentials pulled in — value partly prioritisation ritual, not only deliverables.

# 3.2 Recruitment & Hiring

## Early Revenue & Growth (~20–70)

### What good looks like

- **Clear multi-input, single-decider model** — three roles, three lenses, **scored not vetoed:**
  - **Hiring manager** — fit with the role as it is now; holds the decision.
  - **One level up** — assesses potential (placement on talent matrix); score, not yes/no.
  - **Culture checker** — scores against a small set of core values (e.g. “strong on entrepreneurship, relatively weak on alignment, 3/5”); score, not kill switch.
- **Hiring manager weighs the three and decides** — “a bit stronger on culture, slightly less potential, fine for the role now — I’m going for it.” Nobody holds a veto.
- **Culture “non-negotiables” are few and owned by hiring manager** — not a blanket “no culture fit” escape hatch.
- **New leader gets the role immediately** — real decisions in their style (above your old bar), with advice in first weeks — not months of partial projects then “slow start.”

### What you actually need to do

1. **Make explicit that 3/5 is a weighting input, not a kill switch** — trust a culture-literate hiring manager to own it.
2. **Run structured feedback:** role fit (HM), potential (manager+1), culture (scored dimensions) → HM decides.
3. **On executive hire:** day-one ownership of domain — stutter-step onboarding is a failure mode of the hirer, not only the hiree.
4. **Accept founder disillusion at scale** — hires at 50+ are a different species than founder types; need specialists (e.g. legal/compliance lead) without expecting clones.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Culture check used as soft veto** — “not a culture match, we really shouldn’t” masks a genuine hiring decision the HM should own.
- **Ambiguity over who the hiring manager is** — recruiters burn out; candidates lost.
- **Founders re-entering late** — “we called so-and-so, we really shouldn’t” — sometimes right, sometimes not, but funnels every hire back to a founder who no longer has context. Parallel: former finance approver still asked to approve costs they no longer understand — “maybe I should just push the button, but I’m not the right person.”
- **Founders structurally disillusioned as company scales** — people hired at 50-strong are simply a different breed than the founders; even the executives they need feel wrong.
- **“We’ll figure out the VP scope after they join”** — four months of project hopping then “not fast enough.”
- **Hiring manager compromises on profile** because market is tight, then delivery blocked — system blamed.

### How this evolves next

- **Founder must hire the first ~50–100; then the decision right has to genuinely move to managers**, or hiring stalls.

# 1.3 Communication Norms (partial · planning visibility)

## Early Revenue → Growth

### What good looks like

- **Leadership communicates priorities and sequence to the whole org** — not only the founder closing funding or running one workstream.
- **CEO narrative + COO cadence** — charisma carries story (“everyone knows it’s these three things, or there’s grief if I chase a fourth”); rhythm carries execution discipline.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Org discovers strategy through side channels** — one founder’s workstream dominates while the rest doesn’t know what’s next for nine months.
- **Single-table founder sync doesn’t scale** — no replacement mechanism for “what we’re doing and why.”

# Cross-cutting · Org design (map mainly to 1.4 + 1.1)

## Growth (~20–70 sized team)

### What good looks like

- **Functional structure for BAU** (marketing ops, dashboards, creatives) **+ cross-functional cells for change/strategy** — not one mode for everything.
- **Priority projects get visible urgency** — external or internal ritual that screams “this matters now.”
- **Accept imperfection** — org design revisits every ~4 months; people leave or want different work; **plan for absence** (don’t staff so lean one sick person collapses the system).
- **Identity supports cross-functional tradeoffs** — e.g. “preview team” building one thing together vs functional tribes that never sync.

### Warning signs you're behind

- **Same high performers in every cross-functional project and every BAU optimisation** — time squeeze + identity squeeze (marketing hat in platform team).
- **Cracks between teams** — dev, marketing, monetization each right locally; platform wrong globally.
- **Believing one perfect org chart** — “the system doesn’t work” when junior given owner role without capability.
