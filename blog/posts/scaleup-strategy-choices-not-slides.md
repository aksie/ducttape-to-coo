*By Stefan Verkerk and Erik Rutten*

This post isn't for strategy geeks. It's for founders and operators who have goals, a roadmap, a nagging feeling that the team is still pulling in slightly different directions — and a healthy dislike of slideware with "Strategy" in the title.

We, Erik and Stefan, are strategy geeks. We admit it. We know how that sounds. Please keep reading anyway. We think the thing you're missing has a name, and that the name has been so badly abused that you've been avoiding it for good reasons.

We'd been circling this post for a while without finding the way in. Then Stefan had lunch with Paul.

Paul co-founded an IT consultancy and grew it to about five hundred people. He hates strategy slideware as much as anyone we know. His company once wrote a booklet about itself; the chapter called "Our strategy" is three sentences long, and mostly says that strategy is something that happens on the work floor while you're busy doing the work. Ask him what won them their market and he'll tell you, with conviction: culture eats strategy for breakfast. So we’ve established he doesn’t like the word Strategy. He is like you.

And yet, reading the rest of that booklet — in the parts not labeled strategy — we found one of the best strategies we've ever seen written down. Real choices, each with a price, each one a refusal a competitor made the other way, all of them holding each other up. His successors still use it, a decade on, to turn down paying work without asking him.

Someone who despised the word and had the thing: this inspired us to this post, that will describe what strategy actually is — not the slideware, but what Paul had and didn't call by its name.

---

## The one thing strategy actually is

Strategy is a set of fundamental choices about how you're going to win — and what you're explicitly *not* going to do.

That second part is the part that gets omitted. It has been said that "The essence of strategy is choosing what not to do." Strategy is not a description of where you want to end up. It's a decision about how you'll get there given your constraints, your context, and your competition — and which paths you're closing off in order to take this one. The reason you need to close off certain paths is because the fundamental choices on how you’re going to win need to be consistent and you need to commit fully to it. You cannot go half-in and you cannot afford to have part of your team following a different approach.

This is why strategy is hard. Not conceptually hard. Emotionally hard. Because real strategic choices mean giving things up. They mean saying no to customers who would pay you, features that would be useful, markets that look attractive. If your strategy doesn't make you a little uncomfortable — if it doesn't involve closing some doors — it probably isn't a strategy.

## Why it matters: 99 small decisions

Nobody builds the wrong company in one decision. They build it in 99 small ones. A designer picks the flow with the fewest clicks for a customer you'd decided not to serve. An engineer builds the integration properly — abstracted, configurable — for a partner you wanted to test cheaply and drop. A salesperson closes the smallest deal because it closes the fastest. Every one of those calls is defensible on its own. None of them get escalated, because they're too small. Together they ship a company that drifts a few degrees off course every quarter.

Goals don't stop that drift; every one of those 99 decisions can be argued to serve a goal. Only a set of choices does — because a choice tells the designer, the engineer and the salesperson which option is *not* ours, at the moment they're picking, without anyone having to ask. Choices that need to be communicated, explained and reinforced all the time, because drift also happens in storytelling (keep this in mind the next time when your team says “do we need to talk about the strategy AGAIN?”)

## The classic example that makes this concrete

In October 1415, Henry V stood with roughly 6,000 to 8,000 exhausted English soldiers at Agincourt, facing a French force of somewhere between 20,000 and 30,000. The French had more men, heavier armour, and a cavalry advantage that should have been decisive. The outcome looked obvious.

The French did what armies with overwhelming advantages tended to do: they prepared for a frontal assault, concentrated their elite men-at-arms at the front for maximum glory and ransoms, and pushed their archers and crossbowmen to the rear where they couldn't slow things down. There was no strategy here. There was a posture — *we have more, so we'll win* — combined with a status game that put the wrong people in the wrong places.

Henry made different choices. He chose terrain: a narrow field hemmed in by woodland, which would compress the French cavalry charge and negate their numbers. He chose to have his men-at-arms dismount and fight on foot, avoiding the risk of heavy horses floundering in rain-softened ground. He chose to wait — to let the French cross the muddy field rather than advance into them. And he positioned his longbowmen on the flanks, protected by sharpened stakes, able to fire into the sides of any cavalry charge.

Each of those was a choice that excluded alternatives. Dismounting the cavalry meant not using cavalry as cavalry. Waiting meant accepting the psychological pressure of facing a larger force. Choosing that field meant fighting on terms that suited a smaller, lighter force rather than a larger one.

The result: the French cavalry charge slowed in the mud, horses panicked under arrow fire, and the compressed mass of dismounted French knights — too many men in too little space — was methodically defeated by a much smaller force. Henry's casualties numbered in the low hundreds. French casualties numbered in the thousands.

This is what strategic choice looks like. Not a vision of winning. An active set of decisions that shape the terms of engagement in your favour, made possible precisely because of what you chose not to do.

## What strategy is not

Paul's dislike of the word is well earned. Most "strategy" work is slideware: written once, presented earnestly, and never consulted again. A deck full of "increase revenue," "improve customer satisfaction," and "be more innovative" is not a strategy — it's a list of things nobody would argue against, which means it helps nobody make a decision. That kind of deck gets called strategy, poisons the word, and gives people who value execution an excuse to skip the real thing.

So before getting to how to build one, it's worth being precise about what tends to get called strategy but isn't.

Goals are not strategy. "Reach €5M ARR by end of year" is a goal. It tells you where you want to be. It says nothing about how you'll get there or why your approach would work against competition. Every company wants to grow. The goal doesn't distinguish you.

Tactics are not strategy. "Launch a LinkedIn campaign," "hire a senior salesperson," "rebuild the onboarding flow" — these are actions. Important actions, possibly, but they're downstream of strategy. Tactics without strategy are a list of things to do with no coherent logic connecting them.

Values and aspirations are not strategy. "We're customer-centric." "We focus on quality." "We move fast." Nobody chooses to be customer-hostile or low-quality. These aren't choices in any meaningful sense because no real alternative was refused.

Doing everything is not strategy. "We serve both SMB and enterprise." "We're building a platform and a services business." "We're competing on price and on quality." When you try to be everything to everyone, you're not making choices — you're deferring them. The result is usually mediocrity across the board rather than excellence anywhere, which is exactly the competitive position you don't want.

Richard Rumelt, in *Good Strategy Bad Strategy*, calls the last category "bad strategy" — and it's the most common kind. It looks like a strategy because it's written down and presented seriously. But it's actually a list of goals dressed up in strategic language, with no coherent account of how those goals connect to each other or to the choices being made about where to compete and how.

## What makes a choice strategic

Not every choice is a strategic one, a *strategic* choice has a specific character. It's differentiating. You're doing something different from competitors, not just doing it better.

WeTransfer didn't try to be a better YouSendIt. It refused the thing YouSendIt was built on: revenue above end user experience: the account and the paywall at the door.

Strategic choices have trade-offs. Peer-to-peer car sharing gave up control of the car, its condition, and the check-in experience. WeTransfer gave up revenue per user. Those were real losses. Both of these worked out because the losses were accepted. A choice that doesn't close off alternatives isn't a strategic choice.

Strategic choices work as a system that creates coherence. WeTransfer's no login, file first, and curated backgrounds were not three decisions but one: end user first. So less friction, so more volume. SnappCar's use of existing cars, widespread availability and focus on rentals where a new car or last-minute check-in is less important all work together to minimize distance and costs, not convenience. Pull out one strategic choice and the others weaken. This is what makes a strategy hard to copy — not any single choice, but the interdependence of all of them.

## Another path

The examples that stay with you have a visible refusal in them. Someone took the obvious road. Someone else took another road, and you can see what that costs.

Peer-to-peer car sharing, as Erik tells it. Every car-sharing pitch deck says the same thing: people have to move, owned cars sit still, cities are full. All true. Yet, car sharing still doesn't spread like wildfire, because it has to be as easy and safe as the car lingering in front of your door — and it puts its price on a receipt, while ownership hides it in depreciation.

The asset path — Greenwheels, Zipcar — owns the cars. Full control, high convenience, but also high costs. So it chose: heavy users, subscriptions, B2B, big cities, short trips, ruthless margin control — on fines, deductibles, buying and selling the fleet, and pricing that is not fully transparent. Not cars everywhere and hope. No wonder lease companies and carmakers took it: it's asset management. There are many advantages from this path: shinier cars that act as rolling billboards for you brand, high convenience, brand advocacy and stickiness with a small group of heavy users, dedicated parking spots. The disadvantage (and risk): high cost and a real challenge when you want to scale beyond high traffic areas and heavy users

SnappCar, and Turo in the US, chose the opposite: peer-to-peer car sharing. Costs solved — the cars already exist, the maintenance and cleaning done by owners, who all see their earnings as bonus income. But: experience broken — older cars, owners who don't reply, more difficult check-in experience, no dedicated parking spots. Renters leave, then owners leave. The fix was a refusal: stop courting the casual lender, build on semi-pro owners who rent for profit, respond fast, keep the car clean, and pay for keyless entry technology. And the best part: leverage their entrepreneurship and local knowledge to decide the right car and rental type for their area — whether a half-day rental of a van where a dent or two more doesn’t matter when you’re moving house or weekend trips with an old Toyota Aygo in a student neighborhood where customers don’t have the money or interest to drive something new. Turo landed in the same place: professional hosts, specialty cars. Even the cheap path had to choose who it was for. You *could* be everywhere and casual lenders are still welcome — but a clear strategy turns the long tail into SEO, not business.

Run it through the rules. *Differentiating:* Greenwheels and Zipcar owned the fleet; SnappCar owned none of it. *Trade-off:* no control over the car, its condition or the check-in — and then saying no to the casual lender who made the network look big. *Coherent:* semi-pro owners, keyless technology, car types and rental duration tuned to the local conditions all fix the same weakness without bringing the costs back onto the platform. *The opposite test:* the asset path made the opposite call and worked too, so this was a choice, not a preference.

WeTransfer, as Stefan tells it. YouSendIt was there first, and it sat on revenue per user: an account, a paywall, maximum revenue creating annoyance, as much as an end user would accept before leaving. A competitor everybody loved to hate. We chose a different path. No login to upload or download. File first, recipient after. Curating the ads and saying no to ugly ads. One bet sat behind all three: Prioritising end user experience in the end would lead to more growth in users, and later revenue, than growing conversion at the cost of end user experience.

That bet had a price. Less revenue per user, and more risk: with free users not paying, the advertising side to free users had to scale up for the business to break even.

Run it through the rules. *Differentiating:* the incumbent sat on conversion; we did the opposite. *Trade-off:* every skipped login and every unsold background was revenue left on the table on purpose. *Coherent:* the three choices are really one choice — less friction, more volume. *The opposite test:* YouSendIt made the opposite call and was a real business, so this was a choice, not a preference. Adoption in the Netherlands and France showed the market was big enough. Growing to about 100 million in revenue on that choice showed it was right.

## How to start building one

The fastest way to test whether you have a strategy is to ask: *what are we explicitly not doing, that a well-funded competitor could reasonably do?*

If you can't answer that, you probably don't have a strategy yet. You have a direction.

Here's a practical starting point. Work through two dimensions:

Externally — what do you do differently in the market?

- Which customers are you *not* serving, that Competitor X actively pursues?
- Write it down specifically: not "we're focused on SMB" but "we don't take clients without an in-house technical lead, while Competitor X will take anyone with a credit card."
- What does that cost you? Which deals do you lose because of this?
- What does it buy you? Why is losing those deals worth it?
- Which problems are you *not* solving, that Competitor Y invests heavily in?
- Again, specifically. Not "we focus on our core product" but "we don't build reporting features, while Competitor Y has half their roadmap committed to them."
- What do you lose? What do you gain?

Internally — how do you work differently from comparable companies?

- How do you build the product in a way that's different from how others in your space do it?
- Specific differences: release cadence, team structure, build-vs-buy decisions, technical choices.
- What are the costs? What are the advantages?
- Where do you *not* invest resources, while comparable companies treat it as standard?
- Not hiring a sales team. Not building an enterprise tier. Not opening a second office. Not doing outbound.
- Why? What does that make possible?

The answers to these questions are the raw material of strategy. What you're looking for is a set of choices that fit together — that reinforce each other — and that reflect a specific theory of how you're going to win. Not a theory that works for every company. A theory that works for *your* company, in *your* market, with *your* constraints and advantages.

Once you have a draft, three tests are worth running:

The opposite test. Could a reasonable competitor make the opposite choice and still succeed? If yes, you have a real choice on your hands — strategy lives in the space where both answers are defensible. If no — if the opposite would be clearly wrong — then you don't have a strategic choice, you have a generally correct preference.

The cost test. What are you giving up? If the answer is nothing significant, the choice probably isn't strategic. Strategy costs something. That cost is what makes it real.

The coherence test. Do your choices reinforce each other? A strategy where the individual choices don't compound is a list of preferences, not a system. The question is whether the choices, taken together, create a position that's harder to replicate than any single element on its own.

## The company that refused the word and had the thing

Back to Paul's booklet. The chapter headed "Our strategy" is three sentences. Strategy emerges on the work floor, it says; focus is the recurring word; choices stay within our specialism. That's it. The founders would have nodded along with everything above about slideware, they clearly did not like the word Strategy. And they weren't a slides company.

The rest of the booklet is where it gets interesting. Under “rules of thumb” and “our view on leadership” sit the actual decisions, the set of coherent actions as Richard Rumelt would call them. No sales or recruitment department — management does that, and management comes from ranks who have done the actual consulting work. No managers hired from outside. No head office, no staff departments; HR, finance and legal are outsourced. Pay is market-rate, no bonuses. Cells of max sixty people, each setting its own goals. Their own academy, half of it people skills. Guest lectures and internships at universities “to show ourselves to future consultants”. People who need “a growing stream of challenges, in size and in complexity”. And one sentence that gives it away: we choose culture as our main differentiator; we pay what the market pays, the difference is what it’s like to work here.

Put those next to each other and they stop being house rules, they are a coherent set of actions. Add the guiding principle and you have a super clear strategy. “Own academy, home-grown managers, recruiting at universities”: you’re hiring young. “No sales department”: the people doing the work sell the next piece of it. “A stream of challenges”: juniors learn fast or leave. Paul said the missing sentence, the guiding principle, over lunch. They hired young, talented people, made them happy, and grew them. You can’t place a 24-year-old on a CV, so secondment was out. They had to sell projects. Harder to sell than seniors by the hour. But it’s the only place a junior learns fast.

Run it through the tests. *Differentiating:* every competitor in that market placed experienced people on the strength of a CV, ran a sales team and a central HR function. *Trade-off:* cold acquisition stayed their weak spot for years; young people on projects are harder to sell than seniors on secondment; all-round managers are heavy roles; and twice the firm walked away from a large, paying client because the work was making its people miserable. *Coherent:* pull one out and the rest sags — without projects no training ground, without training no home-grown managers, without those no cells. *The opposite test:* the conventional model was a perfectly good business. This was a choice.

So the founders had a strategy, specific enough that their successors still turn down work with it a decade later, without asking. They just never filed it under that name.

## Can you duct tape a strategy?

Back to the question behind this series: how does this translate to each maturity phase of a company?

We believe there is no fix for a lack of sharp thinking on how you’re going to win, and on how what you do today fits the company you’re trying to become. You either have a coherent set of choices or you don’t. There’s no half set.

So you need a strategy, but it can be duct-taped together.

First: It can be short. A strategy doesn’t need the analysis, the competitive deep-dive or the deck. Paul’s fit on a few pages, between photos of a company outing. At the kitchen table it may be one sentence: “if we can build this and get the cost down, we have a business.” That’s a guiding policy. It counts.

Second: It should change. Early on, your time horizon is months, not years. You haven’t been pinned to a business model or a customer segment yet, and the market may move faster than you can write it down. Treat the strategy like a prototype: cheap, explicit enough to test, and dropped without ceremony when it’s wrong. Polishing it makes you attached to it.

But you can’t skip the thinking. Short is fine. Vague isn’t. “We’ll figure it out as we go” is a plan to have 99 small decisions made for you.

And between changes it has to be clear to the whole team. A strategy that lives in the founders’ heads works at five people and quietly stops working at fifteen. Whatever you call it — founder intuition, principles, values — the guiding policy and the actions that follow from it have to be known by everyone making decisions in your name. Change it as often as you need to. But at any given moment, there should be one version, and everyone should know it.

As you are growing, building the strategy goes from duct tape to a real four-storey building. Early on, the job is just to get the core right and align the handful of people around you, including your advisors, then get back to validating it. Later, your management team, employees and investors will expect a strategy, your choices get harder to reverse, and there are more interests to balance — so communication, repetition and constructive challenge become real work, because saying no or changing course gets expensive once you have something to lose.

We both like the size best where it is complex enough to need the real thing, free enough to shape it and execute it.

And as a bonus, you can call your strategy whatever you like. Right, Paul? ;-)
