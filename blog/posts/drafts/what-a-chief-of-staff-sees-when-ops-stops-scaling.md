I spent a morning with a chief of staff. We organised the conversation around the same themes I use in the [diagnostic](https://aksie.github.io/ducttape-to-coo/diagnostic.html): **planning**, **communication**, **decision rights**, and the boring social work of **ownership** — not as a checklist, but as a readable essay.

What follows is **their** voice, in **first person**, edited for clarity and anonymity. They start where the break begins — when the lunch table isn't big enough — and end on what they think actually fixes it. I find it super useful; it is not universal law. The wiki describes what good tends to look like at a stage; this is one person's craft at roughly **fifty to three hundred people**, unless noted otherwise.

*— Guest author: chief of staff (anonymous pending review). Everything below this line is their voice.*

---

## When the lunch table stops being enough

Up to about nine or eleven people, strategy and pricing can still be a conversation at the same table — alignment by osmosis. Before you hit Dunbar-ish limits, I'd **split teams deliberately** rather than wait for politics to force it. The "one lunch table" myth outlasts the org chart in the founder's head long after it's physically impossible.

Past that, founders who feel everyone naturally works toward the whole are often wrong: they see the whole; new hires see a slice. Around **fifty people**, five teams can run five different rhythms without anyone naming that the shared picture is gone.

In the first ~15 hires, I'd still prioritise **diversity of thinking** over personality frameworks — hard to retrofit later. Survive product-market fit first.

What I watch for in planning: one function runs a heavyweight goal process copied from a book while another executive ignores the page; key results invented from solutions, not problems; tech and commercial living in different planning languages.

## Planning rhythm at scale

**How I run planning** — my synthesis, not a branded framework:

| Layer | Horizon | Where it lives |
|-------|---------|----------------|
| **Strategic** | ~12 months — problems and chances | Annual / rare deep forums |
| **Tactical** | Quarter | Quarterly planning |
| **Practical** | Weeks | Weekly forums |

Match meeting cadence to layer. Weekly is for practical work — I avoid strategic debates there; nobody is prepared, decisions get rushed. Decisions should largely be made **before** the meeting; the meeting confirms.

I separate **running metrics** (company top line — MRR, NPS, eNPS, whatever you use), set from C-level with input on feasibility, from **change goals**: each department owns a small set of real problems or chances (I use three to five), with solutions at year horizon, then quarter.

Departments should **pre-book only ~70%** of capacity on their own objectives. The rest is slack for cross-team dependencies — marketing needs engineering, engineering needs legal. When two departments collide, C-level resolves priority. Departments at 100% local load and permanent gridlock are a pattern I watch for.

At the largest scale I've worked, I've hung **posters per department** — strategy visible quarters ahead, readable by other teams. Old school; it works for me.

## Getting strategy to the whole company

Information mostly **cascades up** fine. Leadership can articulate precisely in the management team. It often **does not cascade down** — teams execute on a stale or partial picture. Strategy lives in the founder's head and the investor deck; the org works from a plan six months old.

I treat internal communication as a **derivative of strategy**, not a separate workstream. If nobody owns downward transmission on the same rhythm as planning, the job isn't finished.

Multi-founder teams explaining direction **slightly differently** to their functions creates unspoken frustration — nobody owns internal comms; everyone assumes someone else is transmitting the strategy.

Founders: telling the same story fifty times in a year feels absurdly repetitive **to you**. In a fifty-person company, each person may have heard it **once**. That's not enough for the organisation; it's barely a start for you.

## How information actually flows

At scale I aim for roughly **80% formal / 20% informal** routes. Informal is good — walk-ins, skip-level — but bounded. Too much informal routing, and two people sit in the same C-level meeting with **100% vs 40% of the information**, a decision gets pressed before everyone's ready, and resentment follows.

In long-tenure organic companies I've seen **~40% informal** routes — people going to the CEO when the line manager is slow or says no. The CEO often feels helpful, decisive, on the move. What gets missed: the line manager's authority was undercut without anyone meaning to, and the org learned to route around process.

I put a **named owner** on streams that would otherwise land on the founder ad hoc — legal and commercial "quick yes/no" questions are the classic case — with a thin layer between "small daily ask" and "CEO intuition."

## Who decides — and who the leader is really playing for

At this stage I recommend leaders play for the **highest team**, not the lowest. A Chief Revenue Officer sits in the commercial team *and* in C-level. The job is to **align** those — not to walk into the C-suite as the commercial delegation's spokesperson. Open disagreement with the CFO can be healthy; secretly carrying your team's vote into the room is not.

I see this with internal promotions and external hires — often from good intentions. People-pleasing managers optimise locally; the company pays in gridlock.

When C-level debates something that isn't theirs to own, I **return it to the domain owner** — *"This doesn't touch revenue; it's the CPO's call"* — and pushback is usually low once the rule is understood.

Founders can be erratic. That's also what got them here. A COO or chief of staff who tries to smooth all of that away will often lose the founder's trust — or the role — because the energy that built the company needs somewhere to go. I wrap **process steps around intuition**: a shower insight becomes a staged test, not a company-wide pivot by Monday.

When conflict rises, I use mediator training — neutral questions, surface what's unsaid. Task-driven, low-empathy founders who believe **the best facts win** and won't move without full conviction are a hard pattern: fine for product, brutal for a growing org. Mediation helps; it doesn't replace willingness to reflect.

## Data, dashboards, and cross-team forums

Each department gets **one dashboard**, at most **five KPIs**, reviewed monthly — a quick read on who is organised and who isn't.

In cross-department forums I've run (on the order of a dozen leaders), the agenda is structured: **need-to-know** for other departments, **red metrics** (target vs actual and why), **blockers and interdependencies**. Discussions **start from data**; if the data isn't there, the meeting's job is to agree to get it — not to decide on intuition alone.

"Half-smart" leaders add complexity to look important; the smart ones simplify.

## Hiring senior leaders

When I hire C-level or senior leaders, I use **three conversations** — my own setup, not a universally agreed way:

1. **Hiring manager** — role fit, hard criteria, chemistry. My Marie Kondo test: did this meeting give you energy?
2. **One level up** — **potential** only: live case, whiteboard, unprepared problem. **No veto** — potential estimate, not final hire.
3. **Long-tenure culture carrier** — coffee, life, work. Also **no solo veto**.

The **three sparks together** decide — not one person's binary "not a fit," which fails often at scale. I use **9-box thinking** (performance × potential × values) as shared language, not bureaucracy.

## When I come in as chief of staff

I wouldn't prescribe a chief of staff at ten people. My trigger list:

- Still the **working foreman/woman** — in the work, not above it enough to prepare
- Arriving at meetings **unprepared**; literally **behind on the facts**, because strategy is losing to the inbox
- Small requests **filling the CEO's calendar** while bigger decisions wait

In the first weeks I listen — about ten days — and walk the org. Then I work on formal routes, CEO preparation, and buffering without leaving the CEO in the dark: roughly a week ahead on strategic conversations; Monday with delegation done and topics known.

In this phase, a COO or chief of staff **shouldn't nudge the founder off everything that still gives them energy** — even when it no longer fits the formal role on paper. If the CEO keeps developing something because that's where they get fuel, I'd protect that deliberately, not treat it as failure to delegate.

## Ownership before you change anything

Planning rhythm, cascades, corridor decisions, who plays for which team — for me it all comes back to the same sentence:

> A process is an efficiency move to get as much relevant information as possible into the heads of the people who make choices.

When alignment breaks, I look first for **who owns the process** — not for "we need OKRs" as the root cause. Early on, founders carrying process implicitly is fine. Later, **everyone's problem** means **nobody's**.

Between fifty and three hundred people, I keep seeing the same gap: an important way of working has **no named owner**. Someone tries to improve planning, communication, or decision rhythm. Daily reality shifts. People lose something. Without a clear owner and trust in the room, the initiative drifts back.

What I aim for:

1. **Every important process has a named owner** — not "leadership," not "everyone."
2. **Change the process only after ownership is clear** — and after the people inside it understand why.
3. **When I leave**, someone else owns the rhythm — a medior chief of staff or equivalent — or the company snaps back to founder intuition within a quarter. I build the machine; I hand it to someone who tolerates repetition better than I do.

Process isn't slideware. It's who carries information to the people who choose — and who keeps carrying it after the consultant, the chief of staff, or the enthusiastic executive goes home.
