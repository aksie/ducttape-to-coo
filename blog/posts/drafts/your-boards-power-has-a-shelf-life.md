Two separate conversations, with two different partners at the same impact-VC fund, kept landing on the same subject: boards. Not board mechanics — decks, pre-reads, cadence, all real but not the point — but what a board actually *is*, and when it actually has power over you. The sources here are investors. Every lesson below is really about what you, as a founder, should expect, demand, and do differently.

## Your board is not a report-card committee

Boards have gotten more professional over the last few years, and more formal along with it. That's good news in itself — the problem is that founders and investors often don't share a picture of what the board is actually there to do. The result: founders who feel like they're working *for* the board, instead of the other way around.

A simple test: is the question in the room "what do you want from us," or "what do I want, and what do I need from you to get there"? The second one is what a good board should be asking — and it's on you to claim it if it doesn't happen on its own.

Disappointing numbers make boards nervous, and the reflex is more control, more scrutiny. For a founder who's already under pressure, that feels like a chokehold. The strongest founders are the ones who can say: "I'm not going to do that — if I do, this happens, and that's not what you want either, is it?"

## Your board's leverage has a shelf life

Here's the part that surprised me most. One partner described a real standoff: a portfolio company badly missing its targets, clearly needing a CEO change and financial discipline it didn't have. The fund's leverage to force that was real — right up until the financing round closed. Once the money was committed, the board could say it disagreed, but it couldn't make the company do anything. The company had to run into the wall on its own first.

That cuts both ways for you. Day to day, your board has less power over you than the org chart suggests — once a round is closed, nobody is going to overrule you into fixing something you haven't decided to fix yourself. But it also means the moment you're actually negotiating — a term sheet, an amendment, a follow-on — is the one moment governance and structure are genuinely on the table. Whatever you don't settle then, you're mostly settling with yourself later, not with your board.

## Bad news on the table, before it has to be

Calling your investor with bad news is one of the hardest things there is — precisely because you depend on them. The temptation is to stack up only the good news. But the honest version of the deal is: put it on the table, and it's shared. Otherwise it's yours alone, later, when it's harder to fix.

The same partner who described the leverage-shelf-life pattern also drew a sharp line between *passive* reporting and *active* relationship-building: don't just wait for the scheduled meeting — a quick call in between, just to check in, does more than the monthly update ever will. Most people won't lie to you outright if you ask directly how things are going; they'll just quietly not volunteer it in the story they've gotten used to telling. Ask directly, more often than feels necessary, and you break that pattern before it costs you.

## The person founders actually call

One fund's stated board philosophy: keep it small — three people, ideally, with at most one shareholder seat — and prefer an external chair. Increasingly, the fund tries to serve only a year or two on a portfolio board itself before handing the seat to a trusted, neutral industry expert who reports back to them.

Ask them why, and the answer isn't subtle: founders go to that person faster than they go to the investor. Partly for the operating experience. Mostly because the investor's economic stake creates a filter founders know is there, whether or not it's ever actually used against them.

If your board offers you that kind of arrangement — a credible, conflict-free operator with a real seat, not just an advisor on the side — take it. And if it doesn't, it's worth building that relationship yourself: someone with real domain experience, no financial stake in your next round, who you'd actually call at 11pm.

## If your board can't find the plan, neither can your team

The clearest single illustration in either conversation. A board pack, real, recent: a full page explaining how an objective and key result *should* be defined — the theory of it, carefully written — and nowhere in the document an actual key result. The owner was in there somewhere, but buried.

The partner's reaction: if I can't find it, and I'm a reasonably sharp outsider whose whole job is reading company plans, the rest of the organization is not going to find it either.

This is the same failure mode as a board deck that creeps toward eighty or a hundred slides — length and procedural correctness standing in for the harder work of actually deciding and stating the target. Run the test on your own next board pack, before anyone else does: hand it to someone outside the company, cold, and ask them to point at the one number that matters and the one name attached to it. If they can't, don't blame the reader.

## What to do with this

None of this is really about your board. It's about what your board can see, and when. A board can only apply real pressure at the table, before the money moves — so use that moment. It can only surface a problem early if you make it easy to reach you outside the ritual of the scheduled meeting — so pick up the phone first. And it can only tell you your plan is unclear by being unable to find the target itself — so hand your own next board pack to a stranger before you send it to your board.
